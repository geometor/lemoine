Exposition of the Theory of Simplicity and Exactitude
=====================================================

.. folio:: 0019
   :page: p. 3

A construction executed with the straightedge and the compass involves only the following elementary operations:

* :term:`R₁ <r1>` — Bring the edge of the straightedge into coincidence with a point :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (R_1)`.
* :term:`R₂ <r2>` — Draw the straight line :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (R_2)`.
* :term:`C₁ <c1>` — Place one point of the compass at a determined point :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_1)`.
* :term:`C₂ <c2>` — Place one point of the compass at an indeterminate point of a line :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_2)`.
* :term:`C₃ <c3>` — Draw the circumference :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_3)`.

(Op. is the abbreviation of the word operation.)

.. picture:: /_static/figures/fig_elementary_operations.svg
   :alt: Diagram of Lemoine's five elementary ruler-and-compass operations R1, R2, C1, C2, C3
   :caption: Lemoine's five elementary operations: straightedge alignment and trace (2R₁ + R₂, S = 3) alongside circle notation O(AB) and locus-constrained transfer (2C₁ + C₂ + C₃, S = 4).

We take no account of the *drawn* length of the lines.

If, for example, we draw a small arc or the entire circle, it is always :math:`C_3`; always :math:`R_2` for any portion of a straight line drawn.

.. _lemoine-en-symbol-coefficients:

Every construction is thus finally represented by:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(l_1 R_1 + l_2 R_2 + m_1 C_1 + m_2 C_2 + m_3 C_3\right).

We call *coefficient of simplicity*, or more briefly *Simplicity of the construction*, the number :math:`l_1 + l_2 + m_1 + m_2 + m_3`, and *coefficient of exactitude*, or more briefly *Exactitude of the construction*, the number :math:`l_1 + m_1 + m_2`, because one readily sees that, in reality, exactitude depends on the preparatory operations :math:`l_1, m_1, m_2` and not on the drawing operations; :math:`l_2` is the number of straight lines drawn, :math:`m_3` the number of circles [*]_ .

To abbreviate the writing, instead of writing: the circumference which has :g:`O` as center and the length :g:`AB` or the length :math:`R` as radius, we shall write: :g:`O(AB)` or :math:`O(R)`.

We shall make here an important remark which applies whenever the general notion of *number* enters into a problem of *Geometrography*;

namely, that the question then leaves the domain of pure *Geometrography* and that arithmology is mingled with it, as will be seen in the sequel of this work. Thus: *To divide a straight line in the ratio of two given lengths* is a problem of pure *Geometrography*, and: *To divide a straight line in the ratio of two given numbers* :math:`m` *and* :math:`n` is not at all in the same case; there is not even any purely graphical *general* method for performing this

.. folio:: 0020
   :page: p. 4

division in the simplest possible manner; each particular case must be studied with regard to the question that supplied those numbers. In practice, one reduces it to the case of lengths by taking along a divided ruler lengths proportional to the given numbers, and one should most often do so, while well knowing that one steps outside pure *Geometrography*,
which permits the use of the straightedge and compass only. To bring the question back to pure *Geometrography*, one would have to lay off any length :math:`m + n` times along a line, etc., and that would depart too far from the construction one executes in practice. Still, if laying off a length :math:`m + n` times along a straight line
so as to mark the divisions :math:`m` and :math:`m + n` is easy, though long and impractical, it is by no means convenient—perhaps not possible—to indicate the means of marking those divisions *as simply as possible* by a general method. The question reduces to the problem: *Given a length, to find a straight line* :math:`m` *times longer.*
Laying off the length :math:`m` times in succession along a straight line is a solution, though not the simplest. In studying the problem, one is led to a question of arithmology wholly analogous to the following, which seems very difficult: *How many multiplications, at least, must be performed to compute* :math:`A^m`, *the number* :math:`A` *being given?*

The question of multiplying a straight line by a number would, moreover, strictly speaking, have required a new symbol to represent the operation consisting in fixing one point of the compass on a given line while the other point is fixed; but owing to the mixed nature of the problems in which it would be used,
and especially because it would depart too far from what one does in practice, we have not dwelt upon this consideration.

There is also one point that merits a few words of explanation, which will answer an objection that I had raised to myself at the outset and which must occur to those who examine our method.
Is it legitimate to suppose the operations :math:`C_1, C_2, C_3, R_1, R_2` identical in order to compose the coefficient of simplicity and the coefficient of exactitude? Obviously not, if in *Geometrography* it were a question of an absolute measure. But this is by no means the case, and I assimilate these operations because they are elementary,
that is to say, indecomposable into simpler ones, and because, speculatively, they are neither *simpler* nor *less simple* than one another. One may, moreover, refrain from this assimilation by contenting oneself with the complete symbol. The word *measure* cannot be exact in the usual sense of that word,
which applies to the comparison of a magnitude with *one* unit of the same nature; a construction is not a magnitude, and it is executed by means of elementary operations irreducible among themselves. If I employ the expression *measure*, it is because I find that it applies better to the aim pursued than the general word comparison.

.. folio:: 0021
   :page: p. 5

Absolute rigor would lead, in many cases, to rejecting any comparison of the relative simplicity of two constructions. Indeed, how could one judge whether the construction :math:`5C_3` is more or less simple than :math:`50R_2`, since the units :math:`C_3` and :math:`R_2` are by essence of different nature;
yet, upon reflection and also with a little practice in *Geometrography*, one will recognize that these assimilations are admissible in the order of exactitude of the drawings themselves; indeed, we draw lines and a line has no dimensions, we place points and a point cannot be marked.

In short, our method provides a speculative *criterion* which has practical applications, and before it none existed. What we do is not a *measure*, it is a comparison with five distinct units: :math:`R_1, R_2, C_1, C_2, C_3`, and one cannot say *in an absolute way* that construction A is simpler than construction B unless the coefficients of all the units are respectively smaller in A than in B, a very frequent case.

.. [*] We have not failed to see that the *simplicity* and *exactitude* of an operation vary in the same sense as the inverse of the numbers we call coefficient of simplicity and coefficient of exactitude; but as no confusion is possible and these are *only names*, we preferred designations recalling the goal to be reached over *coefficient of complication* and *coefficient of inexactitude*, certainly more logical.
