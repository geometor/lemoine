Problems XI–XVI: Copying Angles & Constructing Triangles
========================================================

.. rubric:: Émile Lemoine — *La Géométrographie* (English Translation, pp. 7–9, fols. ``0023–0025``)

.. folio:: 0023
   :page: p. 7

**XI.** — *Through a given point* :g:`B` *on a straight line* :g:`BC`, *to draw a second straight line making with the first an angle equal to a given angle* :g:`DAE` [*]_ .

I trace the circle :g:`A(AE)` of arbitrary radius which cuts :g:`AD` at :g:`D`, :g:`AC` at :g:`E` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_3)`;
then the circle :g:`B(AE)` which cuts :g:`BC` at :g:`F` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_3)`.

I take the length :g:`DE` with the compass, then I trace the circle :g:`F(DE)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`, which cuts :g:`B(AE)` at :g:`H`.

I trace :g:`BH` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`.

Symbol of the total operation: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + 3C_3)`; simplicity 11; exactitude 7; 1 straight line, 3 circles [*]_ .

**XII.** — *Knowing the angles* :math:`\alpha` *and* :math:`\beta` *(whose vertices I also call* :math:`\alpha` *and* :math:`\beta`) *of a triangle, to construct the third* :math:`\gamma`.

I trace an arbitrary straight line :g:`AB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (R_2)`.

I trace with an arbitrary radius :math:`R` the three circumferences :math:`\alpha(R)`, :math:`\beta(R)`, :g:`O(R)`,

:g:`O` being any point of :g:`AB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_2 + 3C_3)`; let :g:`B` be the point where :g:`O(R)` cuts :g:`AB`.

I take the length of the chord :g:`CD` that :math:`\alpha` intercepts on :math:`\alpha(R)` and lay it off at :g:`E` from :g:`B` on :g:`O(R)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.

I take the length of the chord :g:`FG` that :math:`\beta` intercepts on :math:`\beta(R)` and lay it off at :g:`H` from :g:`E` (in the direction :g:`BE`) on :g:`O(R)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.

I trace :g:`OH` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`; the angle :g:`HOA`, :g:`A` being on :g:`AB` on the opposite side of :g:`O` from :g:`B`, is the required angle.

:math:`\mathrm{Op.} : (2R_1 + 2R_2 + 8C_1 + 6C_3)`; simplicity 18; exactitude 10; 2 straight lines, 6 circles [*]_ .

   :page: p. 8

**XIII.** — *To construct a triangle knowing one side* :math:`a` *and the two angles* :math:`xoy, x'o'y'` *adjacent to the side* :math:`a`.

I trace a straight line :g:`BC` and on this straight line, from an arbitrary point :g:`B`, I take :math:`BC = a` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (R_2 + 2C_1 + C_2 + C_3)`.

I trace :math:`o(BC)`, :math:`o'(BC)`, :g:`C(BC)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 3C_3)`.

On :math:`o(BC)` I take the chord :math:`xy` intercepted by the angle :math:`xoy` and carry it from :g:`C` to :g:`C'` on :g:`B(BC)` which was traced to obtain :g:`C`; likewise on :math:`o'(BC)` I take the chord :math:`x'y'` and lay it off from :g:`B` to :g:`B'` on :g:`C(BC)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (6C_1 + 2C_3)`.

I join :g:`CB'`, :g:`BC'` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`, which intersect at :g:`A`.

:g:`ABC` is the required triangle.

In all: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 3R_2 + 11C_1 + C_2 + 6C_3)`; simplicity 25; exactitude 16; 3 straight lines, 6 circles.

It is clear that if one performs the construction either upon the given side or by taking one of the given angles

as an angle of the required triangle, the symbol of the construction will be simpler.

In the first case, one will not need to take the length :math:`a`, nor to trace a straight line, nor to lay off :math:`a` upon that line, and the circles traced from :math:`o, o'`, :g:`C` as centers will be traced with an arbitrary radius :math:`R`,

but it will be necessary to trace :g:`B(R)` in addition; the symbol will therefore be:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 10C_1 + 6C_3),

and, in the second case:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 8C_1 + 4C_3).

**XIV.** — *To construct a triangle* :g:`ABC`, *knowing the side* :math:`AB = c`, *the side* :math:`AC = b` *and the angle* :math:`BAC = xoy`.

I trace an arbitrary straight line :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (R_2)`.
From an arbitrary point :g:`A` upon this straight line, I take :math:`AC = b` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_2 + C_3)`.

.. folio:: 0025
   :page: p. 9

I trace :math:`o(AC)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_3)`.

I take :math:`xy` and I trace :math:`C(xy)` which cuts :g:`A(AC)` at :g:`B'` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`;

I trace :g:`AB'` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`.

I take the length :math:`c` which I lay off as :g:`AB` upon :g:`AB'` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`; I trace :g:`CB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`.

Symbol: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 3R_2 + 9C_1 + C_2 + 4C_3)`; simplicity 21; exactitude 14; 3 straight lines, 4 circles.

**XV.** — *To construct a triangle knowing two sides* :math:`a` *and* :math:`b` *and the angle* :g:`B` *opposite to one of them.*

One finds for the two solutions, when the solution is possible:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : (6R_1 + 4R_2 + 9C_1 + C_2 + 4C_3);

simplicity 24; exactitude 16; 4 straight lines, 4 circles.

**XVI.** — *To construct a triangle knowing the three sides.*

One finds: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 3R_2 + 8C_1 + C_2 + 3C_3)`; simplicity 19; exactitude 12; 3 straight lines and 3 circles.

.. [*] We always suppose, *in our standard constructions*, that the sheet upon which we execute them contains only the given data. These data are separate, and one does not execute the construction upon one of them,

   except when that follows from the question. Thus, if I wish to construct a fourth proportional to three given lines, I suppose that the three lengths are *separate* upon the sheet and that one does not perform the construction upon one of them. If, on the contrary, one seeks the center of gravity of a given triangle,

   it is clear that one operates upon the triangle, and it is most often so when one applies our theory to a definite problem; the standard constructions employed are then simplified by reason of the operations that are already done, which one consequently does not have to count.

.. [*] When we do not explain the constructions,

   they are the classical constructions given from all time in geometries; we have taken them then from the *Traité de Géométrie* of MM. Rouché and de Comberousse, 6th edition.

.. [*] I will remark here that in my memoir of the Congress of Oran, 1888, p. 82, I had clumsily directed this construction to which I assigned

   the symbol :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 3R_2 + 10C_1 + 6C_3)`; indeed, I had uselessly drawn the straight line which I here call :g:`OE` and I had drawn in two stages the circumferences which gave me the angle :math:`BOE = \alpha` and then the angle :math:`EOH = \beta`, that is to say I had done uselessly:

   :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 2C_1)`. A like remark applies to several constructions of that same Oran memoir, and it is not surprising that it should be so, for although the theory of simplicity was formulated, I did not yet know how to apply it. For this reason, as I say in the introduction,

   I give anew the symbols of the fundamental operations while simplifying them when occasion offers, and also because, being far from suspecting then that nearly *all* the fundamental constructions given since *Euclid* in elementary geometries were too complicated—sometimes by a little, sometimes by half—this apparent repetition allows me to give simpler constructions

   which ought logically to become the classical constructions. It is astonishing that such simple didactic questions, placed at the beginning of Geometry, should remain insufficiently studied after so many generations; hence when chance led me to make this remark, I was greatly surprised, yet I explained it to myself, because geometers, having no criterion

   in this matter, concerned themselves only with simplicity of expression, with the obvious link between a theorem and a construction they indicated, without systematically turning their attention to the practical part of execution and to the reasoned conditions of its simplicity. For instance, in an enunciation: *to join the poles of two lines* is as quick to say and forms as simple a phrase as: *to join a given point to the vertex of an angle*, yet, compass in hand, it is quite different, since one must first construct the poles, etc.
