Problems LIV–LXIII: Brocard, Steiner, Tarry, Gergonne, Spieker, Nagel & Neuberg
===============================================================================

.. rubric:: Émile Lemoine — *La Géométrographie* (English Translation, pp. 45–52, fols. ``0061–0068``)

.. folio:: 0061
   :page: p. 45

**LIV.** — *Place a* :term:`BROCARD point <points-de-brocard>`.

The direct point :math:`\omega` for example, such that :math:`\omega AC = \omega CB = \omega BA`.

I shall rely upon the construction of the *Brocard* angle given by M. :person:`Brocard <brocard>` (*A. F.*, Algiers Congress, 1881, 10, p. 146).

I describe three circles of the same arbitrary radius :math:`R`, :g:`A(R)`, :g:`B(R)`, :g:`C(R)` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 3C_3)`.

Through :g:`A` I draw the parallel :g:`X'AX` to :g:`BC` by making, by means of equal arcs taken on circles :g:`A(R)` and :g:`C(R)`, angle :math:`CAX' = ACB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + C_3)`.

I make at :g:`B` (on the opposite side of :g:`AB` from point :g:`c`) angle :math:`ABX = ACB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + C_1 + C_3)`.

I trace :g:`CX` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`.
:g:`CX` contains :math:`\omega` and :math:`XCB` is the *Brocard* angle.

By means of the arc it intercepts on :g:`C(R)`, I trace, transferring it onto :g:`B(R)`, etc., the straight line :math:`B\omega` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + C_3)`, and I obtain point :math:`\omega` by the symbol:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 10C_1 + 6C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 28; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 6\text{ circles}.

.. folio:: 0062
   :page: p. 46

**LV.** — *Place both* BROCARD *points* :math:`\omega` *and* :math:`\omega'`.

Having made the same constructions as before, I trace two of the straight lines :math:`A\omega'`, :math:`B\omega'`, :math:`C\omega'`, by means of two of the circumferences :g:`A(R)`, :g:`B(R)`, :g:`C(R)` and the chord of the arc (which I hold in the compass) intercepted on one of them by the *Brocard* angle :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 2C_1 + 2C_3)`.

I have therefore placed both *Brocard* points by a construction whose symbol is:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(12R_1 + 6R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 38; \quad \text{exactitude } 24; \quad 6\text{ lines}, 8\text{ circles}.

These are the simplest constructions for obtaining the *Brocard* points... until simpler ones are indicated, if any exist.

**LVI.** — *Place the* :person:`STEINER <steiner>` *point.*

I make use of the following proposition (*Mathesis*, 1889, p. 69, in the article which is the translated reproduction of the chapter from: *A Sequel to the first six books of the Euclide*, by M. J. :person:`CASEY <casey>`, 5th edition).

*From the vertices* :g:`A, B, C` *of the triangle as centers with respective radii* :math:`a, b, c`, *I describe circles which intersect two by two on the circumscribed circle at* :math:`A_1, B_1, C_1`; :g:`BC` *and* :math:`B_1 C_1` *intersect at* :math:`A_2`; :math:`AA_2` *cuts the circumscribed circle at the point of* STEINER.

I trace :g:`A(BC)`, :g:`B(CA)`, :g:`C(AB)` which intersect two by two at :math:`A_1, B_1, C_1` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (9C_1 + 3C_3)`.
I trace :math:`B_1 C_1` which cuts :g:`BC` at :math:`A_2`, then I find :math:`AA_2`, :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

I trace :math:`C_1 A_1` which cuts :g:`AC` at :math:`B_2`, then I trace :math:`BB_2`, :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

:math:`AA_2` and :math:`EB_2` intersect at the *Steiner* point, which is thus given by the symbol:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 24; \quad \text{exactitude } 17; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles}.

**LVII.** — *Place the* :person:`TARRY <tarry>` *point.*

I trace the circumscribed circle :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 5C_1 + 4C_3)`.

I trace the circle :g:`A(BC)` which cuts the circumscribed circle at :math:`B_1` and :math:`C_1` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.

I trace :math:`B_1 C_1` which cuts :g:`BC` at :math:`A_2`, I trace :math:`AA_2` which cuts the circumscribed circle at point :g:`R` of *Steiner* :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

I trace the diameter :g:`RO` of the circumscribed circle :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)`; the opposite extremity to :g:`R` is the *Tarry* point.

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 8C_1 + 5C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 28; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 5\text{ circles}.

.. folio:: 0063
   :page: p. 47

Let us remark that when one thus places the *Tarry* point, the *Steiner* point finds itself previously placed by the symbol :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (8R_1 + 4R_2 + 8C_1 + 5C_3)`, a simple symbol, yet slightly less so than the one we have just given.

**LVIII.** — *Construct the length* :math:`\frac{S}{R}` *or* :math:`\frac{2S}{R}` *in a triangle.*

Let :g:`A'` be the foot of the altitude dropped from :g:`A` onto :g:`BC`; :math:`A'_c` and :math:`A'_b` the projections of :g:`A'` onto :g:`AB` and onto :g:`AC`; we shall have :math:`A'_b A'_c = \frac{S}{R}`.

The intersection of :g:`B(BA)` and :g:`C(CA)` will give :math:`A_1` symmetric to :g:`A` with respect to :g:`BC`; let us trace :math:`AA_1`; we shall have :g:`A'` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)`.
The intersection of :g:`B(BA')` and :g:`A(AA')` will give :math:`A'_{1c}` symmetric to :g:`A'` with respect to :g:`AB`; let us trace :math:`A' A'_{1c}`, which will cut :g:`AB` at :math:`A'_c` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)`.

We shall similarly have :math:`A'_b` by :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)`, and :math:`A'_c A_b` (which need not be traced), that is to say :math:`\frac{S}{R}`, is obtained by:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(6R_1 + 3R_2 + 12C_1 + 6C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 27; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 3\text{ lines}, 6\text{ circles}.

If one remarks that :math:`A'_{1c} A'_{1b} = \frac{2S}{R}`, one perceives that :math:`\frac{2S}{R}` can be constructed more simply than :math:`\frac{S}{R}`; indeed, I construct :g:`A'` as before by :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)`, then :math:`A'_{1c}` and :math:`A'_{1b}` by operating thus:

I construct :g:`A(AA')`, then :g:`B(BA')` which cuts :g:`A(AA')` at :math:`A'_{1c}`, then :g:`C(CA')` which cuts :g:`A(AA')` at :math:`A'_{1b}` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (6C_1 + 3C_3)`.

In all:

.. math::

   \mathrm{op.} : \left(2R_1 + R_2 + 10C_1 + 5C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 18; \quad \text{exactitude } 12; \quad 1\text{ line}, 5\text{ circles}.

I give this construction LVIII only as a very simple example of the remarks that the application of a construction can prompt, and also to show the usefulness of retaining in memory, or of noting down, the value of a fairly large number of elements of the triangle, in order to shorten constructions when the occasion arises.

Had I not used the value of :math:`A'_c A'_b`, I would have constructed :math:`\frac{S}{R} = \frac{a h_a}{2R}` only by a much more complicated symbol; it would have been necessary, for example: to draw the altitude starting from :g:`A`, to construct the circumscribed circle in order

.. folio:: 0064
   :page: p. 48

to have the diameter radius :math:`2R`, and finally to seek the fourth proportional between :g:`BC`, the altitude, and :math:`2R`.

In our first construction of :math:`\frac{S}{R}`, instead of drawing the perpendiculars from :g:`A` onto :g:`BC` and from :g:`A'` onto :g:`AB` and :g:`AC` by placing their symmetrics, I could have used the classical construction XX *a*, and I would have obtained the line :math:`\frac{S}{R}` by the symbol :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 9C_1 + 9C_3)`, of the same simplicity, yet requiring more lines;
let us remark, moreover, that I could not then have constructed :math:`\frac{2S}{R}` as simply as I did.

**LIX.** — *Place the* :person:`GERGONNE <gergonne>` *point of one of the circles tangent to the three sides of a triangle.*

The construction indicated (*A. F.*, Paris Congress, 1889, p. 213, § 7) gives:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 27; \quad \text{exactitude } 19; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles},

to place a single point, and:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(22R_1 + 11R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 45; \quad \text{exactitude } 31; \quad 11\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles},

to place all four of them.

**LX.** — *Place the center of gravity* :g:`I` *of the perimeter.*

I make use of the first construction indicated (*A. F.*, Paris Congress, 1889, p. 205, § 5).

I trace :g:`B(a)`; then :g:`A(a)`, which cuts :g:`AC` at :math:`\beta` in the direction of :g:`AC` and :g:`AB` at :math:`\gamma` in the direction of :g:`AB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 2C_3)`.
I take the midpoint :math:`\gamma'` of :math:`B\gamma` and the midpoint :math:`\beta'` of :math:`C\beta` by means of the three new circles :math:`\gamma(a)`, :g:`C(a)`, :math:`\beta(a)`, since :g:`B(a)` is already traced :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 3C_1 + 3C_3)`.

I trace :math:`C(C\beta')` which cuts :g:`CB` at :math:`\beta_1` in the direction :g:`CB` and :math:`B(B\gamma')` which cuts :g:`BC` at :math:`\gamma_1` in the direction :g:`BC` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 2C_2)`.

Finally, I trace :math:`\beta'\beta_1`, :math:`\gamma'\gamma_1` which intersect at :g:`I` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

In all:

.. math::

   \mathrm{op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 10C_1 + 7C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 29; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 7\text{ circles}.

By continuous transformation in :g:`A`, one would place in an analogous way the point :math:`\frac{b+c}{a}`, :math:`\frac{c-a}{b}`, :math:`\frac{b-a}{c}`.

**LXI.** — *Place the* :person:`NAGEL <nagel>` *point:* :math:`\frac{p - a}{a}`, *etc.*

Let :math:`a > b > c`.

I trace :g:`A(a)` which cuts :g:`AC` at :math:`\beta` in the direction :g:`AC` and :g:`AB` at :math:`\gamma` in the direction :g:`AB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.
I trace :math:`C(C\beta)` which cuts :g:`CB` at :math:`\beta_1` in the direction :g:`CB` and :math:`B(B\gamma)` which cuts :g:`BC` at :math:`\gamma_1` in the direction :g:`BC` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 2C_3)`.

I trace :math:`\beta\beta_1` and :math:`\gamma\gamma_1` which intersect at the *Nagel* point, :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(4R_1 + 2R_2 + 7C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 16; \quad \text{exactitude } 11; \quad 2\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles}.

One easily verifies this construction of the *Nagel* point, because the equations of :math:`\beta\beta_1` and :math:`\gamma\gamma_1` are respectively:

.. math::

   \begin{aligned}
   a^2 x - b^2 y + cz(a - b) &= 0, \\
   -a^2 x + by(c - a) + c^2 z &= 0,
   \end{aligned}

straight lines which intersect at the *Nagel* point.

One would place by an analogous construction deduced from the preceding by *continuous transformation* in :g:`A`, in :g:`B`, and in :g:`C`, the continuous transforms: :math:`-\frac{p}{a}`, :math:`\frac{p - c}{b}`, :math:`\frac{p - b}{c}`; etc., of the *Nagel* point.

**LXII.** — *Place the point* :math:`\Phi` : :math:`\frac{a^2 b^2 + a^2 c^2 - b^2 c^2}{a}`, *etc.*

We have frequently encountered this point (see *J. E.*, 1883, Problem VII, p. 243; *A. F.*, Grenoble Congress, 1885, § 2, p. 28; *A. F.*, Toulouse Congress, § 2, 3, 4°, p. 23, etc.); it is also, as we have shown, the radical center of the three circles of :person:`Neuberg <neuberg>`. :math:`\Phi` is the point where
the two Brocardians of the *Lemoine* line intersect with respect to the line at infinity.

We shall construct it starting from the following property:

*If* :g:`A'` *is the symmetric of* :g:`A` *with respect to the midpoint of* :g:`BC`, :math:`A_1` *the foot of the symmedian starting from* :g:`A`, :math:`A' A_1` *passes through* :math:`\Phi`.

I place :g:`A'` and :g:`C'` as follows:
I take :g:`AC`; I trace the parallel to :g:`AC` drawn through :g:`B` by drawing a rhombus whose side has length :g:`AC`, resting on the straight line :g:`AC`, with one vertex at :g:`B`;
the points :g:`A'` and :g:`C'` are thus placed by the intersections of this parallel and of the circle :g:`B(AC)` which served to trace it :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + 3C_3)`.

This requires that I have chosen for :g:`B` a vertex such that :g:`AC` is greater than the altitude starting from :g:`B`. By means of circles :g:`C(CC')`, :g:`A(AA')`, I

.. folio:: 0066
   :page: p. 50

take
on the arcs they subtend between the sides of angles :g:`C` and :g:`A` the symmetrics :g:`C''` and :g:`A''` of :g:`C'` and :g:`A'`, with respect to the bisectors of angles :g:`C` and :g:`A`; :g:`C''` and :g:`A''` lie on the symmedians starting from :g:`C` and from :g:`A`.

These symmedians cut :g:`AB` and :g:`CB` at :math:`C_1` and :math:`A_1` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 10C_1 + 4C_3)`.

I trace :math:`C' C_1`, :math:`A' A_1` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

These straight lines intersect at point :math:`\Phi`, thus obtained by the symbol:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 15C_1 + 7C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 37; \quad \text{exactitude } 25; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 7\text{ circles}.

**LXIII.** — *Place the point* :g:`W` : :math:`\frac{a^4 - b^2 c^2}{a}`, *etc.*

This point has also presented itself to us very often. (*N. A.*, 1885, § 1, no. 4, p. 204; *A. F.*, Limoges Congress, 1890, p. 124; Marseille Congress, 1891, p. 155, no. 13; see, regarding this last reference, the cross-reference indicated to construction *b* given further on, of the problem before us.)

I shall first place this point by making use of the values of its coordinates; I shall then give another, simpler method.

*a)* To reduce the given coordinates of :g:`W` to straight lines, I shall divide all three by the same quantity, which must be the product of two straight lines.
I choose the product :math:`bc` of two sides of the triangle, which seems to me to permit the greatest possible reductions in the construction; these coordinates can then be written:

.. math::

   \left(\frac{a^2}{c} \cdot \frac{a}{c} - \frac{bc}{a}\right), \qquad \left(\frac{b^2}{c} - \frac{a^2}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{b}\right), \qquad \left(\frac{c^2}{b} - \frac{a^2}{c} \cdot \frac{b}{c}\right).

We must therefore first construct the lines :math:`\frac{a^2}{b}`, :math:`\frac{c^2}{b}`, :math:`\frac{b^2}{c}`, :math:`\frac{a^2}{c}`, :math:`\frac{bc}{a}` which, for brevity, I call respectively :math:`l_1, l_2, l_3, l_4, l_5`.

One could do construction :math:`\mathrm{XXXIV}^{\textit{bis}}` five times for this, but savings are possible.

1° For all five constructions, I have to trace only three circles having centers :g:`A, B, C`, taking them of the same radius; this will save :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (7C_1 + 7C_3)`.

2° To obtain :math:`\frac{a^2}{b}`, :math:`\frac{c^2}{b}`, I have to make angles :g:`A` and :g:`C` at :g:`B`;
to obtain :math:`\frac{b^2}{c}`, :math:`\frac{a^2}{c}`, angles :g:`A` and :g:`B` at :g:`C`; to obtain :math:`\frac{bc}{a}`, angle :g:`C` or angle :g:`B` at :g:`A`.

(Refer to the detail of the cited construction.)

I shall therefore take between the legs of the compass only once the chord

.. folio:: 0067
   :page: p. 51

corresponding, in the three traced circles, to angles :g:`A, B, C`, since these circles are traced
and I shall then be able to use, for the constructions of the angles, the chord of an angle at the moment when I hold it between the legs of the compass; I shall thereby save :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1)`.

I have thus reduced the solution to constructing the point :math:`\left(l_1 \frac{a}{c} - l_5\right)`, :math:`\left(l_3 - l_1 \frac{c}{b}\right)`, :math:`\left(l_2 - l_4 \frac{b}{c}\right)`, having as yet performed only :math:`5[2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + 3C_3] - 7C_1 - 7C_3 - 4C_1`, or :math:`\mathrm{op.} : [10R_1 + 5R_2 + 14C_1 + 8C_3]`.

To take the three fourth proportionals :math:`l_1 \cdot \frac{a}{c}`, :math:`l_1 \cdot \frac{c}{b}`, :math:`l_4 \cdot \frac{b}{c}` which I call :math:`\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3`, I operate thus:

*Construction of* :math:`\lambda_1` *or* :math:`l_1 \cdot \frac{a}{c}`:

I lay off :g:`AB` as :g:`CL` on :g:`CB` in the direction :g:`CB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.

I lay off :math:`l_1` as :g:`CL'` on :g:`CA` in the direction :g:`CA` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`; then I shall draw through :g:`B` a parallel to :g:`LL'` (without tracing :g:`LL'`), construction XVII.

3° This parallel will cut :g:`CA` at :g:`L''` and :g:`CL''` will be :math:`\frac{l_1 a}{c}` or :math:`\lambda_1` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)`.

*Construction of* :math:`\lambda_2` *or* :math:`l_1 \cdot \frac{c}{b}`:

Through :g:`L'`, I draw a parallel to :g:`AL` (without tracing :g:`AL`) :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)`, which cuts :g:`CB` at :g:`N`; :g:`CN` will be :math:`\lambda_2`.

*Construction of* :math:`\lambda_3` *or* :math:`l_4 \cdot \frac{b}{c}`:

I lay off :math:`l_4` on :g:`CB` as :g:`CP` in the direction :g:`CB` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)`.

Through :g:`P`, I draw a parallel to :g:`LA` (without tracing :g:`LA`) :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)`, which cuts :g:`CA` at :g:`P'`; :g:`CP'` will be :math:`\lambda_3`.

I have now to construct the three lengths :math:`\lambda_1 - l_5`, :math:`l_3 - \lambda_2`, :math:`l_2 - \lambda_3`.

Which I do by laying off :math:`l_5` on :math:`\lambda_1`, :math:`\lambda_2` on :math:`l_3`, :math:`\lambda_3` on :math:`l_2` in the proper direction by :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (9C_1 + 3C_3)`, and I finally have three lengths :math:`\mu_1, \mu_2, \mu_3` by the symbol :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (16R_1 + 8R_2 + 50C_1 + 20C_3)`.

It will only remain for me, to place :g:`W`, to execute construction XLIX of the point whose proportional normal coordinates are :math:`\mu_1, \mu_2, \mu_3`, and I shall have obtained it by the total symbol:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(32R_1 + 16R_2 + 68C_1 + 32C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 148; \quad \text{exactitude } 100; \quad 16\text{ lines}, 32\text{ circles}.

.. folio:: 0068
   :page: p. 52

*b)* I shall rely, to carry out the second construction, upon the means of constructing the point :math:`V_1 : x' x_1 (y' z_1 - z' y_1)`, etc., knowing the points :math:`M' : x', y', z'` and :math:`M_1 : x_1, y_1, z_1`, a means which I gave at the Marseille Congress, *A. F.*, 1891, p. 155, no. 13 [*]_.

One sees that if :g:`M'` and :math:`M_1` are the *Brocard* points, :math:`V_1` is the point :math:`W : \frac{a^4 - b^2 c^2}{a}`, etc., with which we are concerned.

I place the *Brocard* points :g:`M'` and :math:`M_1` by construction LIV :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (12R_1 + 6R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3)`.

Since, in this construction, I traced only two of the straight lines :g:`AM'`, :g:`BM'`, :g:`CM'` and two of the straight lines :math:`AM_1, BM_1, CM_1`, I trace the other two, which are necessary for me here :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_1)`.

I place the point called :math:`\alpha` (*loco citato*) :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`, and I trace the straight line :math:`A\alpha`, which contains :g:`W`, and the straight line :math:`B\beta`, which also contains :g:`W` :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (8R_1 + 4R_2)`.

:g:`W` is then placed by means of the symbol:

.. math::

   \mathrm{Op.} : \left(28R_1 + 14R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 62; \quad \text{exactitude } 40; \quad 14\text{ lines}, 8\text{ circles}.

And nothing says, naturally, that by relying on other properties of point :g:`W`, one might not find something better.

I treated this question especially to give an example of the way to discuss problems of construction; I will add that constructions drawn from the theorems of the geometry of the triangle (such as construction *b* of this point :g:`W`) are, so to speak, always much simpler than those deduced simply from the value of the coordinates
of the point to be constructed, however much care one takes, moreover, as I have done here, to economize constructions by taking advantage of all simplifications that the nature of the data suggests.

All these *very simple* remarks, which are made quickly and easily as soon as one has a little habit
of constructing according to our principles, are, as one sees, very long and rather tedious to detail, precisely because of their degree of obviousness; in following this memoir, pencil in hand, one will see that it reads without any effort and that almost everywhere the reader's thought will immediately follow
or even anticipate our exposition, for the knowledge required by the theory properly so called of *the art of constructions* is confined to the first three books of the Geometry of *Legendre*.
