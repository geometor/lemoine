Complementary Note & Analytical Summary (d'Ocagne, Laisant & Complete Index)
============================================================================

.. rubric:: Émile Lemoine — *La Géométrographie* (English Translation, pp. 60–66, fols. ``0076–0082``)

.. folio:: 0076
   :page: p. 60

My friend M. :person:`Maurice d'Ocagne <docagne>`, who kindly presented this memoir to the Section of Mathematics, wrote me a gracious letter regarding it from which I extract the following lines: 
"... I believe that from the point of view of pure speculation, a solution capable of being summed up in briefer language will always be preferred to another, even if the latter were simpler in the absolute sense you give to that word; one must indeed note 
that most constructions indicated in pure geometry are destined never to be actually realized—such are constructions of centers of curvature for curves other than the usual curves; it is therefore better that they be expressed in a more concise, more elegant form, rather than translated into a more expeditious graphical operation. 
That is far, moreover, from taking away the interest attached to the ingenious considerations you have developed; these indeed find a vast field of application in practical geometry and especially in descriptive geometry. The art of drafting drawings has everything to gain by drawing inspiration from your methods... 
I share with you these reflections which I offered to our colleagues of the First Section, so that you may reply to them..."

I thank M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` doubly, both for having presented this work for me and for having written me these lines; yet I have no 
need to answer as regards his observation, for *I am entirely of the same opinion as he* and had no thought of doing or saying anything that implied another; I will only take this opportunity to specify my aim clearly. I am not at all surprised 
that this aim did not stand out for M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` from a reading of this long memoir, which could not be thorough since I handed it to him the evening before his departure for Pau, and moreover I fear *especially* that I did not bring this aim into sufficient relief.

I am not concerned at all with the exposition of geometry; for each question, the more concise, elegant, etc. it is, the better, that is obvious, and there is nothing to change in the ideal of perfection the geometer must pursue; I aim at something else, for, *alongside the*

.. folio:: 0077
   :page: p. 61

*speculative* solution of a question, there is the effected construction of that solution, and the way of realizing constructions constitutes a particular branch of knowledge, an art with which no one has ever occupied himself; it alone is what my work is about.

I do not even claim to follow the real construction exactly, since I assume as a hypothesis that the instruments and the drawing sheet have all possible dimensions up to infinity, that the relative positions of the data are indifferent, etc. It is *rational construction* that I analyze; one cannot, I believe, analyze 
*in a general way* the real construction, since the execution of one and the same construction is either easy or practically impossible according to the sizes or positions of the data. Thus it is often easy to place the intersections of a straight line and a circle—one need only trace them 
on the drawing; but if the circle has a radius of 100 meters, how shall we manage?

We therefore cannot follow the construction actually carried out, yet it is clear that of two constructions of the same problem, both evaluated by our method, the one having the smallest number 
of elementary operations to execute will be *by essence* the simplest, and that, all else being equal, it is the one that should rationally be put into practice rather than the one requiring a greater number of operations for its realization; in the *very frequent* case where 
one compares two constructions and, in one of them, all the coefficients of :math:`R_1, R_2, C_1, C_2, C_3` are respectively *at most* equal to the coefficients of the other, the matter is not even open to debate.

There is a single point in M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>`'s letter 
on which we perhaps do not agree: it is when he says that geometric constructions are, at bottom, only speculative—that is, that one never executes them. That was true for the Greeks; if they drew figures as sketches on sand, the thing served merely to aid reasoning, 
but it was not construction. This explains why they, so refined, so ingenious in their geometric speculations, had no thought at all of *Geometrography*, which had no object since they did not make drawings [*]_ ; we say a construction made by means of straightedge and compass, 
the Greeks said a solution possible with the straight line and circle: our expression indicates the instruments of construction, theirs the speculative data. The so simple and natural idea of *Geometrography*

.. [*] The Greeks did not make drawings *even for their*
   *constructions of buildings*; this at least is the opinion of the scholars who have specially studied the question, of M. *Choisy*, for instance, whose fine works on Greek architecture are well known; all dimensions were determined by calculation;
   moreover, had they made some sketches on the ground, on wall surfaces, etc., that had little relation to our drawings and could not give rise among them to the idea of a *proper art* of geometric construction.

.. folio:: 0078
   :page: p. 62

was not born earlier precisely because geometry comes to us from the Greeks, because we naturally followed in their footsteps, adopted their methods, developed their conceptions, etc., without imagining that at the foundation there lay a detail of which they could not have thought, since its object—effective geometric construction—did not exist 
for them. Today, *Geometrography* is needed, on the contrary, for people practically use many geometric constructions—and of the most delicate kind—in precision workshops, for machines, etc., etc. I must say, moreover, that this utilitarian point of view did not guide me; I simply thought that, since it is deemed useful 
to give constructions that can be executed with straightedge and compass, one had to give the simplest possible ones and also indicate the general means of constructing most simply. To show completely that geometric drawings of speculative origin are actually executed in various quarters for practical purposes, 
and that there are even geometers led to execute them for their researches, would carry me too far, yet I wish to cite a few examples in support of my assertion.

1° In the course of a research, one often has a presumption of a theorem; 
the proof of its truth or falsity may lead either to very long calculations or to studies all the more tedious to attempt as they are made in pure loss if the presumption is not true; many geometers therefore find it convenient to save time by first making 
a practical verification by drawing, that is, a construction whose result will *prove* nothing, of course, but will ordinarily indicate whether the idea should be pursued or abandoned; I have myself had recourse to this procedure quite often.

2° I will cite next a short paper by M. :person:`Laisant <laisant>`: 
*Constructions graphiques de nombres transcendants*, inserted in the book published on the occasion of the centenary of the Société Philomatique, in 1888, which *obliged* a delicate construction for which he had to turn to a skillful draftsman.

3° Important speculative results have even been *discovered* 
by the *sole* means of graphical constructions and *proved* afterward; why should preliminary trials be powerless in the hands of the geometer, when the arithmologist makes them an ordinary means of arriving at the probability or falsity of the theorem he has in view? Moreover, here is 
a case I cite in detail because I believe it little known. M. *Dunesme*, former student of the École des Beaux-Arts, architect, master of graphical drawing at the École Normale and the former Lycée Napoléon, who died some twenty years ago, discovered, compass in hand, very curious propositions; I point out the following among those he

.. folio:: 0079
   :page: p. 63

communicated to the Institute and which are now standard theorems:

**a)** *Every curve* :math:`C` *is the shadow of a surface of revolution* :math:`S` *(illuminated by parallel rays) onto a plane perpendicular to the axis of* :math:`S` ; *the evolute of* :math:`C` *is the shadow of a conoid having as axis* 
*the axis of* :math:`S`, *as director plane the plane perpendicular to that axis, and as directrix the shade line (ombre propre) of* :math:`S`.

**b)** *If a conic is rotated about an axis parallel to an axis of figure, it generates a surface of revolution whose shade line projected onto a plane perpendicular to the axis is a conchoid of a conic.*

**c)** *If a sinusoid is rotated about the line of centers, it generates a surface of revolution* :math:`S` ; *if this surface is illuminated by rays at* :math:`45^\circ`, *the shade line of* :math:`S` *projected onto a plane perpendicular to the axis consists of two circles; the cast shadow on the plane perpendicular to the axis is a cycloid.*

M. *Dunesme* executed admirable drawings with meticulous care, determining :math:`R_1` and :math:`C_1` with a magnifying glass, etc.; I have these details from my comrade *H. Laurent*, entrance examiner at the École Polytechnique; M. *Dunesme* was a close relative of his mother.

4° M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` himself—very slightly—felt the influence 
of *Geometrography*. Toward the end of 1891, at a meeting of the Mathematical Society, he spoke to us of a geometric construction problem suggested by the studies of his present service (the General Leveling of France), and indicated a solution; the same day, I presented in this connection 
a succinct summary of the studies I was making to evaluate the simplicity and exactitude of geometric constructions. At a following meeting M. :person:`Laisant <laisant>` brought a simpler solution of the same problem, and M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` a modification of the first which seemed, however, evidently less simple 
to construct than M. :person:`Laisant <laisant>`'s and actually was so, as my method of comparison demonstrated. M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` then returned to the same question, for he had M. *Bouquet de la Grye* present to the Academy of Sciences a new solution which he believed, *wrongly*, 
simpler, no doubt because it was stated more briefly and he had no other criterion.

Intending to draft, as an application of my method, a note which I shall soon present to the Mathematical Society and in which I shall compare all these solutions of the same problem from the standpoint 
of the simplicity and exactitude of construction, I asked M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` for some details and, in his reply, he sent me a latest solution which I have just examined and which, indeed, is the simplest of all. I truly believe that, without the idea of *Geometrography*, 
this problem would not have been treated so thoroughly, for any geometer who had not had

.. folio:: 0080
   :page: p. 64

this new preoccupation would have been satisfied with the first solution.

This example shows further that, even when one's attention is drawn to the simplicity of constructions, one cannot, without our method, judge which are the simplest, for M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` had evidently believed that the solution 
presented to the Academy was simpler than the one he had first expounded at the Mathematical Society, and the contrary is true in the most absolute manner.

Save for this slight restriction, I can only subscribe to what M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>` said, things which I moreover intended 
to indicate in several places of the present memoir.

There is a point that also merits a few words of explanation, which will answer an objection I made to myself at the outset and which must immediately occur to those who examine our method. 
*Is it legitimate to suppose the operations* :math:`C_1, C_2, C_3, R_1, R_2` *identical in composing the coefficients of Simplicity and Exactitude?* No, evidently, if *Geometrography* were concerned with a kind of absolute measurement; yet such is in no way the case, and if I assimilate these operations, 
it is because they are elementary—that is, indecomposable into simpler ones—and speculatively neither simpler nor less simple than one another. The word *measure* therefore cannot be rigorously introduced in the sense it usually has, since it applies to comparing one magnitude with another magnitude of the same nature taken as unit; 
a construction is not a magnitude and is executed by means of elementary operations irreducible to one another. If I use the expression: measures of simplicity, etc., it is in a figurative sense, because I find it suits my purpose better than the general word: comparison. 
To demand absolute rigor here is impossible and would be absurd, for it would lead to rejecting even any comparison between the practical simplicities of certain constructions; how, indeed, can one rigorously judge whether the construction :math:`2C_3` is simpler or less simple than :math:`50R_2`, since the units :math:`C_3` and :math:`R_2` 
are different. By reflecting a little on the essence of the question and practicing *Geometrography*, one will recognize, I think, as we do, that our assimilations are admissible in the order of speculative exactitude in which geometric drawings themselves are admissible, for we say: I draw a line, I place a point, 
and neither the line nor the point has objective existence. There are, moreover, *very frequent* cases where even these theoretical scruples would not apply at all; thus the construction whose symbol is :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 8C_1 + 3C_3)` is, from whatever point of view one takes, less simple 
speculatively than the one whose symbol is :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + C_3)`, since the coefficients of all the elementary operations, which are in reality the independent units of our evaluation, are smaller in the

.. folio:: 0081
   :page: p. 65

latter than in the former; this case occurs, for example, in M. :person:`d'Ocagne <docagne>`'s problem, the problem of which we have just spoken; finally, our method gives, in all cases, a more or less perfect speculative *criterion* whose incontestable practical results we have already shown in this memoir; 
before it, no criterion existed.
