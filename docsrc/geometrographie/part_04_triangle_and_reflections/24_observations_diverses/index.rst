Various Observations: Idealizations, Oran Notation & Replies to Mannheim and Brocard
====================================================================================

.. rubric:: Émile Lemoine — *La Géométrographie* (English Translation, pp. 52–60, fols. ``0068–0076``)

.. folio:: 0068
   :page: p. 52

I have treated this question especially to give an example of how construction problems are discussed; I will add that constructions drawn from theorems of the geometry of the triangle (like construction *b* of this point :math:`W`) are, so to speak, always much simpler than those deduced merely from the value of the coordinates of the point to be constructed, 
whatever care one takes, moreover, as I have done here, to economize the constructions by taking advantage of all simplifications suggested by the nature of the data.

All these *very simple* remarks, which are made quickly and easily as soon as one has some habit of constructing with our principles, are, as can be seen, 
quite long and somewhat tedious to detail, precisely because of their degree of obviousness; in following this memoir, pencil in hand, one will see that it reads without any effort and that almost everywhere the reader's thought will immediately follow or even anticipate our exposition, since the knowledge required by the theory properly so called of 
*the art of constructions* is confined to the first three books of Legendre's Geometry.

I have occasionally repeated various observations; I have not done so unintentionally, for the subject treated being new, I thought it well to insist thus

.. folio:: 0069
   :page: p. 53

on certain details whenever they arose naturally in several places, so as not to compel the reader to remember everything that had been said previously.

We did not have as our aim, in studying the Simplicity and Exactitude of constructions, to create something that would correspond exactly to practical cases; indeed, we believe the thing 
impossible for many reasons: for example, in any theory whatever, one can only count equally the intersection of any two straight lines, the intersection of a circle and a straight line, etc.; and if, in a drawing, one of the lines lies wholly off the paper, if the circle has a considerable radius, 
if the two lines nearly coincide, etc., etc., the operations are, in reality, sometimes impracticable, sometimes very difficult; thus the appreciation of all the diverse combinations that can present themselves in this way evidently escapes all measurement. From the fact that our measures do not correspond to immediate reality, one cannot conclude the sterility 
of the method, any more than—*si parva licet componere magnis*—one can say of rational mechanics that it is useless because it does not correspond to practice. Moreover, this work alone, in which the fundamental constructions of geometry admitted for centuries are effectively simplified by our method, suffices to establish its utility, for it is hard 
to believe that if geometers' attention had been drawn in this direction, they would have put, as if for pleasure, from all antiquity, complicated types of construction into didactic treatises, had they thought simpler ones existed.

We have made the following hypotheses:

- All circles are equally easy to draw.
- All straight lines are equally easy to draw.

That is to say, we operate on an infinite sheet and the size of compasses and rulers is unlimited.

It is in the same spirit that we reasoned in assigning the same symbol :math:`C_1` to the operation consisting in placing the point of a compass at a point :math:`A` when one of the points 
is free and to the operation consisting in placing the second point of the compass at a point :math:`A` when the first is held at another point :math:`B`—an operation performed to take, between the legs of the compass, the distance separating the two points :math:`A` and :math:`B`.—We considered only this: *in both cases* 
we bring a point into coincidence with a determined point, not concerning ourselves with the *maneuver* to which the instrument obliges us for this; moreover, one may remark that if the *maneuver* is indeed different, the care required to perform the two operations is the same, if one wishes to obtain the greatest possible exactitude. In such a

.. folio:: 0070
   :page: p. 54

theory, one will always stand between pure speculation and facts, since there is no real representation of a point, what we consider as such being a small surface, either on the drawing sheet or at the tip of the compass, etc.

It might still seem necessary to take account 
of the number of times the construction obliges us to change instruments by putting down the compass to pick up the straightedge and vice versa; one would employ a new symbol for this—the thing would, moreover, be easy—but it seems superfluous to us and does not lie in 
the point of view we have adopted; first, this change of instrument is neither a preparatory operation :math:`R_1, C_1, C_2`, nor a drawing operation :math:`R_2, C_3`, that matters to the result; second, the idea that would admit it is the desire to take account of time, 
and we do not consider that element directly. We say that construction :math:`A` is simpler than construction :math:`B` if :math:`A` requires fewer theoretical elementary operations than construction :math:`B`, that is all.

The positions of the data bring *in practice* impossibilities or complications of drawing 
to resolve difficulties; then time would evidently be an element to consider, but we believe it impossible to do so theoretically; one may also object that the time spent on preliminary study of the construction to be executed offsets what would be gained by executing 
the drawing without so much searching, but first a little practice makes this examination rapid and, above all, we do not consider *time*, but have in view *the exactitude* of the drawing, which is evidently greater the fewer operations there are to perform, since each entails an error [*]_ .

.. [*] Regarding the influence of the number of operations on the final exactitude of the result, we will point out a question that seems very interesting to us, but which we have not pursued, because its solution depends on speculations with which we are not very familiar.
   I call :math:`E` the probable mean error made on each elementary operation, :math:`E_n` the final probable error of a construction whose simplicity is :math:`n`. This granted, what is the probable value of :math:`\frac{E_n}{E_{n'}}` if the same result is sought by two solutions requiring respectively :math:`n` and :math:`n'` elementary operations, that is, whose coefficients of simplicity are :math:`n` and :math:`n'`?

It is still in following the same theoretical idea that we adopted the hypothesis that the *elementary* operations :math:`R_1, R_2, C_1, C_2, C_3` were equal in forming the coefficient of simplicity; we consider them as *elements* and an operation of simplicity :math:`n` is an operation requiring :math:`n` elementary operations.

It would be easy to imagine means that would *seem* to evaluate the ratios of the duration of elementary operations by having several *determined* constructions executed at the same time by groups of good draftsmen, who would repeat the same construction :math:`m` times, marking the time and deducing therefrom, by taking the coefficients of :math:`R_1`,

.. folio:: 0071
   :page: p. 55

:math:`R_2`, etc. as unknowns, equations allowing their duration ratios to be determined; yet upon a little reflection, one sees that one would thus obtain only values applying to the particular circumstances of the drawings adopted for this experiment, and in no way to general practice; 
the thing may however have an interest of curiosity, although we do not make time enter directly into *the art of geometric construction*, and we plan to carry it out if we find favorable circumstances for doing so.

I have already said that geometers had hitherto sought only 
the speculative simplicity of reasoning and expression, and had not seemed to suspect that the simplicity of real construction was quite another thing.

This evidently comes from the fact that geometers generally construct little, and *the art of construction* has hitherto had no place in geometry: 
1° because speculative geometers have never occupied themselves with it; 2° because professional draftsmen generally have very little need for these subtleties in the usual constructions of their craft; that they must keep their minds applied more to practice properly so called than to theoretical researches (yet 
usable by them and that they have adopted, without examination and quite naturally, the constructions indicated from all time by geometers in the didactic books in their hands).

It is not at all surprising that the simplicity of speculative reasoning does not correspond *very frequently* to the simplicity of construction:

1° Because the geometric lexicon often allows condensing very complex operations into a single word;

2° Because reasoning is free from all restraint, whereas construction is bound to use certain specific instruments, the straightedge and compass [*]_ , by means of which everything must be executed.

.. [*] We have not considered the :term:`set-square (équerre) <equerre>` here because it is not used in constructions that must be very exact; yet, as we have shown (*A. F.*, 1888, Congress of Oran, p. 9 and elsewhere), it is easy to evaluate the symbol of operations where that instrument is employed.

When the idea first occurred to us to take up these questions, we first thought of another representation of constructions, of which we shall say a few words.

With a straightedge one can do nothing else, for a construction, than:

- Draw an arbitrary straight line: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\rho_1)`;
- Draw a straight line passing through one given point: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\rho_2)`;

- Draw a straight line passing through two given points: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\rho_3)`.

And, with a compass, nothing else than: to take between the legs of the compass the distance of two points: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\gamma_1)`.

To transfer this distance onto a given line:

.. folio:: 0072
   :page: p. 56

- Either from an arbitrary point: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\gamma'_1)`.
- Or from a given point: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\gamma''_1)`.
- Draw a circle with an arbitrary center: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\gamma_2)`.
- Draw a circle with a given center: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (\gamma'_2)`.

Any drawing made with these instruments can therefore be represented by a symbol of the form:

.. math::

   A(\rho_1) + B(\rho_2) + C(\rho_3) + D(\gamma_1) + E(\gamma'_1) + F(\gamma''_1) + G(\gamma_2) + H(\gamma'_2).

We abandoned it fairly quickly:

Because this representation is too complicated;

Because these diverse operations are too different from one another to be assimilated from any point of view;

Because most of the symbols it admits are composed of irreducible operations which it is better to take as starting points;

Because it does not bring out the preparatory operations :math:`C_1, C_2, R_1`, and deals only with tracings;

Because one cannot take as rational a point of view as the one we have adopted above;

Because it gives no notion of evaluating Exactitude, and because, despite the detail into which it seems to enter, it is worth less than the representation that would content itself with saying: this drawing requires so many straight lines, so many circles.

I wish to have shown clearly by this memoir 
that there exists an *art of geometric constructions* which has its own rules, its elegance, its great didactic value as an exercise in discussion, and finally its practical application.

As a completion of the ideas put forth in the Oran Congress memoir already cited, it would remain to recast geometry 
by putting all propositions into the classical form of the syllogism. We even believe that this is the most important part of the subject—of which what precedes is only a particular application—because it is the sole means of bringing out beyond dispute all the irreducible elementary notions 
or experimental axioms that serve as the foundation of geometry and which, in short, are always debated as soon as one deals with them philosophically; we regret being unable, at least for now, to undertake this study, which is of first-rate interest in our opinion.

I have said 
in the course of the work I have just submitted to your judgment: *Geometers have never concerned themselves with constructions down to their final material execution.* It is certain that, upon reading this sentence, a protest against this assertion will come to geometers' minds: *why, on the contrary, it is the final aim of theorems and one*

.. folio:: 0073
   :page: p. 57

*always occupies oneself with it.* I do not doubt that this reflection will be made, for it has never failed to be the reply to my statement when I put it forward in conversation. I do not think I can better refute it and prove my thesis than by citing here 
*(with the assent of the geometers involved)* two typical facts:

In the month of November 1891, at a meeting of the Mathematical Society, I had spoken of my ideas on *the art of geometric constructions*, and I was conversing on this subject with M. :person:`Mannheim <mannheim>` as we left.

I am delighted to be able to cite M. :person:`Mannheim <mannheim>` on this occasion, for no geometer has better than he—with an evident preoccupation—elegantly given, on the subjects that have occupied him: wave surface, radii of curvature, triangular-thread screws, construction of the axes of an ellipse given two 
conjugate diameters, memoir on geometrical optics, kinematic geometry, and in his lectures at the École Polytechnique, etc., clear and simply *expressed* final constructions.

Here are fragments of our conversation relating to the object I have in view:

I.—"... The geometer calls simple a construction synthesized in 
a few words of the geometric vocabulary; but, compass in hand, the simpler of two constructions is not the one explained with the fewest words; thus, for the construction of the problem of *Apollonius*, of which I spoke this evening, one must, in the solution of *Bobillier* 
and :person:`Gergonne <gergonne>`, find the radical center of the three circumferences, which requires drawing two radical axes, etc., and it is necessary, in order to know whether the solution of *Bobillier* and :person:`Gergonne <gergonne>` is the simplest to draw, to occupy oneself first with seeking the simplest drawings it involves, 
that of the :term:`radical axis <linie-der-gleichen-potenzen>` of two circumferences, etc...."

M. MANNHEIM.—"... There are several very simple means: I will cite, at first sight, the property of the radical axis passing through the midpoints of the lengths intercepted on the common tangents between the two circles..."

The *Geometer* was right; for him, in his speculations, when two circles are given, the common tangents are given, the midpoints of the segments as well, etc.; he uses them in his reasonings and draws his construction statements from them; he stops, his task is finished 
as soon as he has reduced the question to elementary geometric constructions.

But the *Constructor*?

Let us examine what he would have to do to draw the radical axis thus, the two circles alone being on the sheet; we will suppose the two circumferences exterior.

1° Draw two of the common tangents to the two circles;

2° Place the points of contact;

.. folio:: 0074
   :page: p. 58

3° Take the two midpoints of the distance separating the points of contact;

4° Finally, join these two midpoints.

Which, taking construction XIX, first method (the simplest in this case), and carrying out the entire construction economically, according to our principles, gives:

:math:`\mathrm{Op.} : (18R_1 + 9R_2 + 19C_1 + 12C_3)`; simplicity 58 
(that is, 58 elementary operations); exactitude 37; 9 straight lines, 12 circles.

And the method we have employed (construction XLI) to draw the radical axis requires only:

:math:`\mathrm{Op.} : (10R_1 + 5R_2 + 2C_3)`; simplicity 17 (that is, 17 elementary operations); exactitude 10; 5 straight lines, 3 circles.

It is more than three times simpler to draw.

It is evident that these considerations will be mere child's play for geometers once their attention is directed to this point; thus, having shown M. :person:`Mannheim <mannheim>`, in the continuation of our chat, that the construction he had cited *at first sight* as simple was very complicated, I was led to say: 
"Well! what is, in your opinion, the simplest construction of the *Lemoine* point?" He no longer answered on the spot as the first time, but sent me the very next morning a construction of the *Lemoine* point which was the same as the one I regarded as simplest and give here (construction LII).

Here is the second fact I wish to cite.

While drafting the text relating to construction LV of this memoir, to place the :term:`Brocard points <points-de-brocard>`, I had the idea of writing to my friend M. :person:`Brocard <brocard>`, asking him to send me whichever construction of these points he believed simplest, 
so as to compare it with the one my method had made me choose.

I copy the relevant passage from his reply.

"For the determination of the points :math:`\omega, \omega'`, it seems to me that the quickest construction is the following, reduced to the minimum of lines.

Let :math:`ABC` be the triangle; draw the circumcircle; draw the three tangents :math:`BC', CB', C'AB'`; join :math:`BB', CC'`, which meet at the *Lemoine* point :math:`K`. Describe the circle :math:`Z` having the straight line :math:`OK` as diameter (:math:`O` center of the circumcircle); 
draw through :math:`A` the straight line :math:`EAD` parallel to :math:`BC`; it meets :math:`BC'` at :math:`E`, :math:`CB'` at :math:`D`; join :math:`DB, EC`, which meet at :math:`A_1` on the circle :math:`Z`; the second intersections of these straight lines :math:`DB, EC` with :math:`Z` are the points :math:`\omega` and :math:`\omega'`."

Let us analyze this construction by executing it to the letter, while taking, however, the *reduced* constructions of this memoir.

1° I draw the circumcircle (see construction XXI): :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 5C_1 + 4C_3)`.

.. folio:: 0075
   :page: p. 59

2° I draw the three tangents at :math:`A`, at :math:`B`, and at :math:`C` (see construction XXV): :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 12C_1 + 9C_3)`.

3° I join :math:`BB', CC'`: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

4° I draw the circle :math:`OK` (construction XIX): :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 3C_3)`.

5° I draw through :math:`A` a parallel to :math:`BC` (construction XVII), making use of construction 1° and noting that the circumcircle already drawn allows me a saving of :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_3)`. :math:`\mathrm{op.} : 2R_1 + 2R_2 + 3C_1 + C_3`.

6° I join :math:`DB, EC`: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)`.

In all: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (22R_1 + 11R_2 + 24C_1 + 17C_3)`; simplicity 74; exactitude 46; 11 straight lines, 17 circles.

Our construction LV gives: simplicity 38; exactitude 24; 6 straight lines, 8 circles.

And yet, had M. :person:`Brocard <brocard>`'s attention been drawn to the point of the *effected construction*, he could have thought only of 
the solution we have developed, for it is, in principle, due to him. (*A. F.*, 1881, Congress of Algiers, 10, p. 146.)

I must add that by applying our method *completely*, one can reduce by several units the symbol of the construction he sent us; indeed, to draw 
the three tangents at :math:`A`, at :math:`B`, and at :math:`C`, one may form at :math:`A` the angle :math:`B'AC = B` using the equal-radius circles described from :math:`A, B, C` in tracing the circumcircle.

One thus has them by: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3)`.

To draw the parallel at :math:`A` to :math:`BC`, one can use the same circles and save two more elementary operations by making angle :math:`EAB = C` while holding the chord length of the arc corresponding to :math:`C` in the compass for tracing angle :math:`B'AC`.

One then has this parallel by: :math:`\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + C_1 + C_3)`.

The points :math:`\omega` and :math:`\omega'` would thus have been given by:

:math:`\mathrm{Op.} : (18R_1 + 9R_2 + 19C_1 + 11C_3)`; simplicity 57; exactitude 37; 9 straight lines, 11 circles.

I did not make these simplifications because they derive too closely from the spirit of the method we have just set forth to suppose that a geometer, however skillful, constructing a figure *as everyone has done hitherto*, would have thought of introducing them; yet, even thus simplified, 
the construction remains much too complicated.

I have cited two examples that seem to me characteristic.

*A duobus discete omnes.*

I believe that everything we have just set forth presents Geometrography as a nearly complete body of doctrine as regards the geometry of the straight line and circle as understood by the Greeks, but two applications remain to be worked out in detail from the modern point of view:

.. folio:: 0076
   :page: p. 60

1° The application to descriptive geometry by adding the use of the :term:`set-square (équerre) <equerre>` and a new elementary operation symbol relating thereto.

2° The application to graphical statics which, besides the set-square, will admit the use of divided rulers to avoid the difficulties arising from 
questions of arithmology introduced by the idea of number, difficulties we pointed out above, for example: regarding the division of a given length into parts proportional to given *numbers* or regarding the construction of a length equal to :math:`m` times a given length.
