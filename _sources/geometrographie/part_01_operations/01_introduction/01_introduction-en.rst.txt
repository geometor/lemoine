Introduction: Aim & Ideal Postulates of Geometrography
======================================================

.. rubric:: Émile Lemoine — *La Géométrographie* (English Translation, pp. 1–2, fols. ``0017–0018``)

.. folio:: 0017
   :page: p. 1

From antiquity onward, geometers have prized elegance and brevity in the *demonstration* of theorems, yet almost universally neglected to measure the simplicity of the *actual execution* of geometric constructions. When :person:`Euclid <euclid>` (:cite:`euclid-heath`) reduces a newly proposed problem to a sequence of previously proved propositions, his criterion of simplicity is strictly *speculative* (:term:`construction spéculative <construction-speculative>`): a solution is judged simple when its verbal enunciation is short and its deductive link to an earlier theorem is immediate. Once one takes straightedge and compass in hand upon the drawing sheet, however, a construction expressed in three lines of prose may require fifty or sixty distinct instrumental acts, whereas a deeper geometric synthesis accomplishes the same point determination in fifteen.

:term:`Géométrographie <geometrographie>`, first sketched by :person:`Émile Lemoine <lemoine>` at the Oran Congress of the *Association Française pour l'Avancement des Sciences* on 30 March 1888 (:cite:`lemoine-1888`), constituted systematically at the Pau Congress in September 1892 (:cite:`lemoine-1892`), and later expanded with restricted-instrument appendices in *Collection Scientia* No. 18 (:cite:`lemoine-1902`), inaugurates an exact quantitative calculus for the art of geometric constructions. Its twofold purpose is:

1. To assign to every proposed ruler-and-compass construction an objective numerical measure of its **simplicity** (:term:`S <simplicite>`) and **exactitude** (:term:`E <exactitude>`); and
2. To replace habitual speculative textbook constructions with genuinely *géométrographique* syntheses that minimize elementary physical operations without sacrificing mathematical rigor.

Methodological Conventions & Ideal Instruments
----------------------------------------------

To separate the intrinsic operational structure of a construction from incidental drafting accidents, :person:`Lemoine <lemoine>` lays down explicit conventions governing both the drawing plane and the canonical instruments (pp. 1–2; elaborated further in *Observations diverses*, pp. 52–54):

* **Unbounded Drawing Sheet**: The sheet of execution is assumed indefinitely extended in every direction; constructions forced to work around torn margins or inaccessible intersections (such as Problems XXIV and XXXVII) constitute special exercises of descriptive drafting (*épure*), not general criteria for elementary simplicity.
* **Ideal Straightedge and Persistent Divider Compass**: The ungraduated straightedge draws an indefinite straight line in a single stroke. Unlike the collapsing compass traditionally inferred from :person:`Euclid <euclid>`'s *Elements* I.1–I.3 (see :external+euclid:doc:`index` and :external+elements:doc:`index`), the modern geometer's compass preserves its aperture once opened between two points until deliberately changed—and the draftsman may set down or use the straightedge without forfeiting the opening currently held between the legs of the compass.
* **Purely Operational Exactitude**: Every alignment of ruler edge or compass point with a determined point or traced curve introduces an elementary observational uncertainty. Leaving physical error propagation and acute intersection angles to graphical calculus (:person:`Maurice d'Ocagne <docagne>`, pp. 60–62), *Géométrographie* counts the discrete alignment acts themselves.
* **Separation of Given Data**: Unless the enunciation expressly states that a construction operates upon an already drawn figure (such as a given triangle or traced circle), given lengths and angles are assumed placed *apart* on the sheet, so that canonical symbols remain strictly comparable across problems.
