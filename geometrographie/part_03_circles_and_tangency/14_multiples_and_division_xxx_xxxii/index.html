Problems XXX–XXXII: Segment Division & Parallels¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 19–20, fols. 0035–0036)

XXX. — To construct a straight line CD that is \(n\) times a given length AB: 1° without marking the intermediate divisions; 2° marking the divisions.

Referring back to VIII, we find:

1° \(\mathrm{Op.} : [R_2 + (n + 2)C_1 + C_2]\).

2° \(\mathrm{Op.} : [R_2 + (n + 1)C_1 + C_2 + nC_3]\).

For certain values of \(n\), one may have simpler particular constructions.

XXXI. — To construct a straight line CD that is the \(n^{\text{th}}\) part of a given straight line AB.

I trace two arbitrary straight lines OH, OL … \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_2)\).

I lay off AB along OH … \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\).

Along OL I take the length OL equal to \(n\) times an arbitrary compass opening and I mark its last two divisions K and L … \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + nC_3)\).

f. 0036 · p. 20

Through K I draw a parallel to LH (without tracing LH) … \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)\) which cuts OH at G; GH is the sought length.

\(\mathrm{Op.} : [2R_1 + 3R_2 + 11C_1 + (n + 3)C_3]\).

Simplicity \(19 + n\); exactitude \(n + 10\) (the \(C_3\) along OL evidently count here, except the next-to-last, in estimating exactitude); 3 lines, \((n + 3)\) circles.

REMARK. — For certain values of \(n\): 2, 3, 4, \(2^p\), etc., for example, one can find simpler particular constructions.

XXXII. — To divide a straight line AB into \(p\) parts proportional to given straight lines \(n_1, n_2, \dots n_p\).

I draw through B a straight line BX … \(\mathrm{op.} : (R_1 + R_2)\) ;

I take on BX, \(BN_1 = n_1\) ; \(N_1 N_2 = n_2, \dots N_{p-1} N_p = n_p\) … \(\mathrm{op.} : [p(3C_1 + C_3)]\).

I trace \(AN_p\) … \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\).

At each of the points \(N_{p-1}, N_{p-2}, \dots N_1\), I make with \(N_p B\) angles equal to \(BN_p A\), \(\mathrm{op.} : [2(p - 1)R_1 + (p - 1)R_2 + (2p + 1)C_1 + (2p - 1)C_3]\).

\(\mathrm{Op.} : [(2p + 1)R_1 + (p + 1)R_2 + (5p + 1)C_1 + (3p - 1)C_3]\) ; simplicity \(11p + 2\); exactitude \(7p + 2\); \((p + 1)\) lines, \((3p - 1)\) circles.

REMARK. — If the parts \(n_1, n_2, \dots n_p\) were too small or too large to be used directly, one would make them all \(\lambda\) times larger or \(\lambda\) times smaller, which we know how to do by operations XXX or XXXI, and one would easily calculate the symbol, which would then be more complicated.

If several parts \(n_1, n_2, \dots n_p\) are equal without all of them being so, and one has several compasses [*] , the general symbol simplifies.

[*]

We always suppose, unless notice is given to the contrary, that only a single compass is used; but there are operations where it is advantageous to have several; this happens if, having taken a certain length with the compass, one still needs that same length later in the construction after having been obliged to disturb the opening of the compass in order to take another length; each time that one is not obliged to make this change of opening, one gains: \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1)\). Let us remark further that although the construction is deduced from geometrical reasoning, the order of the constructions need not follow the order of that reasoning. Thus, if the reasoning shows at various parts of its development that several circles of the same radius whose centers are already fixed have to be constructed when one constructs the first, one will evidently have to describe them all while one has that radius in the compass opening, etc.; therefore it is necessary, for any carefully executed construction, to study it beforehand as a whole, to examine it by a sort of reasoned sketch in order to arrive as simply as possible at the sought result; there is a veritable art of geometrical constructions with which one has never systematically concerned oneself; the geometer, as I have already remarked, says as simply: “I take the polar of A with respect to circle O” as he says: “I join the two points A and B,” and the thing executed is quite different. The geometer seeks the simplicity of the phrase, of the deduction, of the idea; if the statement of the construction he indicates is simple, he says: “The construction is simple”; it is with this simplicity alone that one has occupied oneself hitherto. The art of geometrical construction or Geometrography places itself at an altogether different point of view.
