Problems XLVII–XLVIII: Catalan’s Square & Axes of an Ellipse¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 34–36, fols. 0050–0052)

We shall complete this study with a few applications chosen somewhat at random and a few remarks that will enable the reader to understand better the spirit and, I hope, the usefulness of our method.

XLVII. — The two extremities A and B of the side of a square being placed, locate the two other vertices C and D.

I describe (fig. 9) AAB, BAB intersecting at K; I describe KAB cutting BAB at G \(\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 3C_3)\).

I draw AG cutting AAB at I \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\).

I draw KKI cutting BAB at C and AAB at D \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\).

In all: \(\mathrm{Op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 4C_3)\); simplicity 13; exactitude 8; 1 line, 4 circles.

We owe this simple construction to M. Eugène Catalan, who told us he had found it in 1847.

By adding the symbol \(\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2)\), it could serve to construct the square ABCD on a given base, very nearly as simply as by the ordinary construction, which can be done — when properly conducted — by the symbol:

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 7C_1 + 5C_3)\); simplicity 21; exactitude 16; 3 lines, 5 circles.

From all that precedes, it is already clear that there truly exists an art of geometric constructions, which we call Geometrography, and which, although never remarked hitherto, rests upon principles of extreme simplicity; its importance lies not principally in the time one can save by practicing it in constructing a figure, which from a certain point of view is a detail, but above all in the greater exactitude it enables one to attain by reducing to a minimum the number of operations to be performed. Finally, it offers the advantage of being a criterion for judging the simplicity of a construction. The need for this criterion will be demonstrated when one observes that most of the constructions celebrated for their simplicity and elegance are not ordinarily the simplest to construct that are known. They were thought simple because they were stated simply in picturing themselves and were remembered without difficulty; we shall cite, for example, the famous construction of M. Chasles, to locate the axes (in magnitude and position) of an ellipse of which two conjugate diameters are placed in magnitude and position; it is far from the simplest to construct; simpler ones were known… without anyone suspecting it.

f. 0051 · p. 35

XLVIII. — Here is this construction as given in the Aperçu historique, note 25; I add to it the letters necessary for understanding what we have to say.

a) Through the extremity \(A\) of one of the two given conjugate semi-diameters, one draws a straight line perpendicular to the second diameter \(OB\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + 3C_3)\); one lays off on this perpendicular, starting from point \(A\), two segments \(AC\), \(AD\) equal to this second diameter \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\); one joins center \(O\) to points \(C\) and \(D\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\); one bisects, by two new straight lines, the angle \(COD\) and its supplement \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 4C_1 + 4C_3)\).

These two new straight lines will be in direction the two principal axes of the ellipse. The sum of the first two lines \(OC\) and \(OD\) will be the major axis of the ellipse, their difference will equal the minor.

The geometer stops there, having indicated constructions whose symbol is \(\mathrm{op.} : (10R_1 + 5R_2 + 10C_1 + 8C_3)\).

But here is what remains for the draftsman to do to fix the axes in their place, in magnitude:

Draw circle \(O(OD)\) cutting \(OC\) at two points \(E\) and \(F\) so as to have the length \(CE\) of the minor axis and the length \(CF\) of the major axis \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\).

Bisect \(CE\) and \(CF\), which, since they have \(C\) as common extremity, can be done by \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 3C_1 + 3C_3)\).

Take the half-lengths thus determined of the axes and lay each off on the proper axis, a very simple choice to make, yet one of which the geometer does not speak \(\mathrm{op.} : (6C_1 + 2C_3)\).

When he stopped, there thus remained to construct \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 11C_1 + 6C_3)\), that is, a little over two-thirds of what had been indicated.

The total construction, economically conducted according to our principles, is summed up by the symbol:

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (14R_1 + 7R_2 + 21C_1 + 14C_3)\); simplicity 56; exactitude 35; 7 lines, 14 circles.

It is clear that one of the things that complicates the application, to the particular construction in question, of the master’s elegant theorem, is that it is the axes that are found by him, and that the semi-axes are needed for the construction, and then their lengths must further be transferred into position on \(OC\) and on \(OD\); it is a detail for the speculative geometer, but by no means for the one who draws the figure.

f. 0052 · p. 36

We propose some day to compare the very numerous solutions that have been given of the same problem in order to determine which is the simplest, and to do the same work for various celebrated problems; meanwhile, we shall give, of this same problem, a solution due to M. Mannheim, which is much simpler than the classical solution of Chasles and which is found in the N. A., 1878, p. 529.

b) Let om, on be the two given conjugate diameters.

From m I drop a perpendicular md onto on (d being on on); I lay off on this perpendicular (in the sense dm) \(me = no\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 4C_3)\).

On \(oe\) as diameter, I draw a circumference whose center is i, and I draw im cutting this circumference at c and at g \(\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 4C_1 + 4C_3)\).

I draw oc, og; these are the axes in position \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

The distances mc and mg (which I do not need to draw) are the lengths of the semi-axes.

I lay off the lengths mc, mg along the respective directions of the axes they represent, and these axes are thus placed in magnitude as well \(\mathrm{op.} : (6C_1 + 2C_3)\).

In all: \(\mathrm{op.} : (12R_1 + 6R_2 + 16C_1 + 9C_3)\); simplicity 43; exactitude 28; 6 lines, 9 circles.

M. Mannheim had not indicated, either, in the article cited, which of the two straight lines oc and og was the major axis; yet he completed the solution (see N. A., 1889, p. 329) by showing that the direction of the major axis is that of the straight line joining o to whichever of the two points c or g bounds the length of the minor axis.

This complete solution is the simplest among those of the same problem whose simplicity we have evaluated, yet nothing proves that none exists or that still simpler ones may not be found.
