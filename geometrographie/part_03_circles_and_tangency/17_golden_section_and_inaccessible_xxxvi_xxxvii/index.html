Problems XXXVI–XXXVII: Golden Section & Inaccessible Intersections¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 27–28, fols. 0043–0044)

XXXVI. — Divide a straight line AB in extreme and mean ratio.

a) By the classical method:

I take the midpoint \(\omega\) of AB and erect at B a perpendicular to AB \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 4C_1 + 4C_3)\).

On the perpendicular to AB drawn at B, I take \(B\omega = BO\) and draw \(\omega(BO)\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 2C_3)\).

I draw \(A\omega\) cutting \(\omega(BO)\) in two points \(l\) and \(m\), \(l\) being between \(A\) and \(\omega\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\).

I draw \(A(Al)\) cutting AB at M between A and B \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\).

\(AM\) and \(BM\) are the sought segments.

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 9C_1 + 7C_3)\); simplicity 25; exactitude 15; 3 lines, [7] circles.

b) Here is a means indicated to me by General Parmentier, but its symbol is somewhat more complicated.

I erect at B a perpendicular to AB and take on it \(BC = 2AB\); the bisector of angle \(CAB\) cuts \(BC\) at \(D\); I take on \(BA\), \(BM = BD\); M is the sought point.

c) The following construction is the simplest I know; it rests upon this theorem: If the length of the tangent drawn from point M to a circle equals the length of a chord AB of that circle, a chord passing through M, \(MA\) and \(MB\) are the greater segments (additive or subtractive) of AB divided in extreme and mean ratio ( \(M, B, A\) following in that order).

I describe (fig. 8) AAB, BAB intersecting at C and C; I describe CAB \(\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 3C_3)\).

I draw through A the tangent to CAB; for this I describe CAB cutting BAB at D and draw AD \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + C_1 + C_3)\).

This is the sought tangent; it cuts AAB at E.

I describe CCE \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\) cutting \(BA\) at M. Since the tangent drawn from M to CAB has the same length as \(AE\), and consequently as AB, \(AM\) is the length of the greater segment of AB divided additively in extreme and mean ratio.

I therefore describe \(A(AM)\), which gives me on AB the sought division point P \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\).

In all: \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 6C_3)\); simplicity 18; exactitude 11; 1 line, 6 circles.

REMARK. — This construction is much simpler than the classical construction; yet it may seem, on looking at figure 8, to be more complicated; this appearance arises from the fact that, in figure 8, we have drawn all the lines used, whereas for the classical figure, to which one is accustomed, one simply says: at B I draw a perpendicular to AB, I lay off on this perpendicular a length equal to half of AB, etc., but none of the auxiliary lines necessary for these operations are drawn on the figure; if one draws them all, the greater complication of the classical procedure leaps immediately to the eye; a similar remark would apply to nearly all the questions we treat in this memoir.

XXXVII. — Draw through a point \(P\) a straight line passing through the point of intersection of two given straight lines that cannot be extended thither.

This problem has received a very large number of solutions. Here is the one whose symbol is the simplest among those I know:

Let \(AA'A''\), \(BB'B''\) be the two given straight lines:

I draw two arbitrary straight lines \(A'B'\), \(A''B''\) intersecting at \(I\), then another arbitrary straight line \(IAB\) passing through \(I\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (R_1 + 3R_2)\).

I draw \(PA'\) and \(PB'\) cutting \(AIB\) at \(E\) and \(F\) respectively; then \(A''E\) and \(B''F\) intersecting at \(P'\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (8R_1 + 4R_2)\).

I draw \(PP'\), which is the sought straight line \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\).

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (11R_1 + 8R_2)\); simplicity 19; exactitude 11; 8 lines.
