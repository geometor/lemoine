Problems LIV–LXIII: Brocard, Steiner, Tarry, Gergonne, Spieker, Nagel & Neuberg¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 45–52, fols. 0061–0068)

LIV. — Place a BROCARD point.

The direct point \(\omega\) for example, such that \(\omega AC = \omega CB = \omega BA\).

I shall rely upon the construction of the Brocard angle given by M. Brocard (A. F., Algiers Congress, 1881, 10, p. 146).

I describe three circles of the same arbitrary radius \(R\), AR, BR, CR \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 3C_3)\).

Through A I draw the parallel XAX to BC by making, by means of equal arcs taken on circles AR and CR, angle \(CAX' = ACB\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + C_3)\).

I make at B (on the opposite side of AB from point c) angle \(ABX = ACB\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + C_1 + C_3)\).

I trace CX \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\). CX contains \(\omega\) and \(XCB\) is the Brocard angle.

By means of the arc it intercepts on CR, I trace, transferring it onto BR, etc., the straight line \(B\omega\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + C_3)\), and I obtain point \(\omega\) by the symbol:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 10C_1 + 6C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 28; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 6\text{ circles}.\]
f. 0062 · p. 46

LV. — Place both BROCARD points \(\omega\) and \(\omega'\).

Having made the same constructions as before, I trace two of the straight lines \(A\omega'\), \(B\omega'\), \(C\omega'\), by means of two of the circumferences AR, BR, CR and the chord of the arc (which I hold in the compass) intercepted on one of them by the Brocard angle \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 2C_1 + 2C_3)\).

I have therefore placed both Brocard points by a construction whose symbol is:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(12R_1 + 6R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 38; \quad \text{exactitude } 24; \quad 6\text{ lines}, 8\text{ circles}.\]

These are the simplest constructions for obtaining the Brocard points… until simpler ones are indicated, if any exist.

LVI. — Place the STEINER point.

I make use of the following proposition (Mathesis, 1889, p. 69, in the article which is the translated reproduction of the chapter from: A Sequel to the first six books of the Euclide, by M. J. CASEY, 5th edition).

From the vertices ABC of the triangle as centers with respective radii \(a, b, c\), I describe circles which intersect two by two on the circumscribed circle at \(A_1, B_1, C_1\); BC and \(B_1 C_1\) intersect at \(A_2\); \(AA_2\) cuts the circumscribed circle at the point of STEINER.

I trace ABC, BCA, CAB which intersect two by two at \(A_1, B_1, C_1\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (9C_1 + 3C_3)\). I trace \(B_1 C_1\) which cuts BC at \(A_2\), then I find \(AA_2\), \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

I trace \(C_1 A_1\) which cuts AC at \(B_2\), then I trace \(BB_2\), \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

\(AA_2\) and \(EB_2\) intersect at the Steiner point, which is thus given by the symbol:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 24; \quad \text{exactitude } 17; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles}.\]

LVII. — Place the TARRY point.

I trace the circumscribed circle \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 5C_1 + 4C_3)\).

I trace the circle ABC which cuts the circumscribed circle at \(B_1\) and \(C_1\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\).

I trace \(B_1 C_1\) which cuts BC at \(A_2\), I trace \(AA_2\) which cuts the circumscribed circle at point R of Steiner \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

I trace the diameter RO of the circumscribed circle \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\); the opposite extremity to R is the Tarry point.

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 8C_1 + 5C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 28; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 5\text{ circles}.\]
f. 0063 · p. 47

Let us remark that when one thus places the Tarry point, the Steiner point finds itself previously placed by the symbol \(\mathrm{op.} : (8R_1 + 4R_2 + 8C_1 + 5C_3)\), a simple symbol, yet slightly less so than the one we have just given.

LVIII. — Construct the length \(\frac{S}{R}\) or \(\frac{2S}{R}\) in a triangle.

Let A be the foot of the altitude dropped from A onto BC; \(A'_c\) and \(A'_b\) the projections of A onto AB and onto AC; we shall have \(A'_b A'_c = \frac{S}{R}\).

The intersection of BBA and CCA will give \(A_1\) symmetric to A with respect to BC; let us trace \(AA_1\); we shall have A \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)\). The intersection of BBA and AAA will give \(A'_{1c}\) symmetric to A with respect to AB; let us trace \(A' A'_{1c}\), which will cut AB at \(A'_c\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)\).

We shall similarly have \(A'_b\) by \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)\), and \(A'_c A_b\) (which need not be traced), that is to say \(\frac{S}{R}\), is obtained by:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(6R_1 + 3R_2 + 12C_1 + 6C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 27; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 3\text{ lines}, 6\text{ circles}.\]

If one remarks that \(A'_{1c} A'_{1b} = \frac{2S}{R}\), one perceives that \(\frac{2S}{R}\) can be constructed more simply than \(\frac{S}{R}\); indeed, I construct A as before by \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 4C_1 + 2C_3)\), then \(A'_{1c}\) and \(A'_{1b}\) by operating thus:

I construct AAA, then BBA which cuts AAA at \(A'_{1c}\), then CCA which cuts AAA at \(A'_{1b}\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (6C_1 + 3C_3)\).

In all:

\[\mathrm{op.} : \left(2R_1 + R_2 + 10C_1 + 5C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 18; \quad \text{exactitude } 12; \quad 1\text{ line}, 5\text{ circles}.\]

I give this construction LVIII only as a very simple example of the remarks that the application of a construction can prompt, and also to show the usefulness of retaining in memory, or of noting down, the value of a fairly large number of elements of the triangle, in order to shorten constructions when the occasion arises.

Had I not used the value of \(A'_c A'_b\), I would have constructed \(\frac{S}{R} = \frac{a h_a}{2R}\) only by a much more complicated symbol; it would have been necessary, for example: to draw the altitude starting from A, to construct the circumscribed circle in order

f. 0064 · p. 48

to have the diameter radius \(2R\), and finally to seek the fourth proportional between BC, the altitude, and \(2R\).

In our first construction of \(\frac{S}{R}\), instead of drawing the perpendiculars from A onto BC and from A onto AB and AC by placing their symmetrics, I could have used the classical construction XX a, and I would have obtained the line \(\frac{S}{R}\) by the symbol \(\mathrm{op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 9C_1 + 9C_3)\), of the same simplicity, yet requiring more lines; let us remark, moreover, that I could not then have constructed \(\frac{2S}{R}\) as simply as I did.

LIX. — Place the GERGONNE point of one of the circles tangent to the three sides of a triangle.

The construction indicated (A. F., Paris Congress, 1889, p. 213, § 7) gives:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 27; \quad \text{exactitude } 19; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles},\]

to place a single point, and:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(22R_1 + 11R_2 + 9C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 45; \quad \text{exactitude } 31; \quad 11\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles},\]

to place all four of them.

LX. — Place the center of gravity I of the perimeter.

I make use of the first construction indicated (A. F., Paris Congress, 1889, p. 205, § 5).

I trace Ba; then Aa, which cuts AC at \(\beta\) in the direction of AC and AB at \(\gamma\) in the direction of AB \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + 2C_3)\). I take the midpoint \(\gamma'\) of \(B\gamma\) and the midpoint \(\beta'\) of \(C\beta\) by means of the three new circles \(\gamma(a)\), Ca, \(\beta(a)\), since Ba is already traced \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 3C_1 + 3C_3)\).

I trace \(C(C\beta')\) which cuts CB at \(\beta_1\) in the direction CB and \(B(B\gamma')\) which cuts BC at \(\gamma_1\) in the direction BC \(\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 2C_2)\).

Finally, I trace \(\beta'\beta_1\), \(\gamma'\gamma_1\) which intersect at I \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

In all:

\[\mathrm{op.} : \left(8R_1 + 4R_2 + 10C_1 + 7C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 29; \quad \text{exactitude } 18; \quad 4\text{ lines}, 7\text{ circles}.\]

By continuous transformation in A, one would place in an analogous way the point \(\frac{b+c}{a}\), \(\frac{c-a}{b}\), \(\frac{b-a}{c}\).

f. 0065 · p. 49

LXI. — Place the NAGEL point: \(\frac{p - a}{a}\), etc.

Let \(a > b > c\).

I trace Aa which cuts AC at \(\beta\) in the direction AC and AB at \(\gamma\) in the direction AB \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\). I trace \(C(C\beta)\) which cuts CB at \(\beta_1\) in the direction CB and \(B(B\gamma)\) which cuts BC at \(\gamma_1\) in the direction BC \(\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1 + 2C_3)\).

I trace \(\beta\beta_1\) and \(\gamma\gamma_1\) which intersect at the Nagel point, \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(4R_1 + 2R_2 + 7C_1 + 3C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 16; \quad \text{exactitude } 11; \quad 2\text{ lines}, 3\text{ circles}.\]

One easily verifies this construction of the Nagel point, because the equations of \(\beta\beta_1\) and \(\gamma\gamma_1\) are respectively:

\[\begin{split}\begin{aligned} a^2 x - b^2 y + cz(a - b) &= 0, \\ -a^2 x + by(c - a) + c^2 z &= 0, \end{aligned}\end{split}\]

straight lines which intersect at the Nagel point.

One would place by an analogous construction deduced from the preceding by continuous transformation in A, in B, and in C, the continuous transforms: \(-\frac{p}{a}\), \(\frac{p - c}{b}\), \(\frac{p - b}{c}\); etc., of the Nagel point.

LXII. — Place the point \(\Phi\) : \(\frac{a^2 b^2 + a^2 c^2 - b^2 c^2}{a}\), etc.

We have frequently encountered this point (see J. E., 1883, Problem VII, p. 243; A. F., Grenoble Congress, 1885, § 2, p. 28; A. F., Toulouse Congress, § 2, 3, 4°, p. 23, etc.); it is also, as we have shown, the radical center of the three circles of Neuberg. \(\Phi\) is the point where the two Brocardians of the Lemoine line intersect with respect to the line at infinity.

We shall construct it starting from the following property:

If A is the symmetric of A with respect to the midpoint of BC, \(A_1\) the foot of the symmedian starting from A, \(A' A_1\) passes through \(\Phi\).

I place A and C as follows: I take AC; I trace the parallel to AC drawn through B by drawing a rhombus whose side has length AC, resting on the straight line AC, with one vertex at B; the points A and C are thus placed by the intersections of this parallel and of the circle BAC which served to trace it \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + 3C_3)\).

This requires that I have chosen for B a vertex such that AC is greater than the altitude starting from B. By means of circles CCC, AAA, I

f. 0066 · p. 50

take on the arcs they subtend between the sides of angles C and A the symmetrics C and A of C and A, with respect to the bisectors of angles C and A; C and A lie on the symmedians starting from C and from A.

These symmedians cut AB and CB at \(C_1\) and \(A_1\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 10C_1 + 4C_3)\).

I trace \(C' C_1\), \(A' A_1\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\).

These straight lines intersect at point \(\Phi\), thus obtained by the symbol:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 15C_1 + 7C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 37; \quad \text{exactitude } 25; \quad 5\text{ lines}, 7\text{ circles}.\]

LXIII. — Place the point W : \(\frac{a^4 - b^2 c^2}{a}\), etc.

This point has also presented itself to us very often. (N. A., 1885, § 1, no. 4, p. 204; A. F., Limoges Congress, 1890, p. 124; Marseille Congress, 1891, p. 155, no. 13; see, regarding this last reference, the cross-reference indicated to construction b given further on, of the problem before us.)

I shall first place this point by making use of the values of its coordinates; I shall then give another, simpler method.

a) To reduce the given coordinates of W to straight lines, I shall divide all three by the same quantity, which must be the product of two straight lines. I choose the product \(bc\) of two sides of the triangle, which seems to me to permit the greatest possible reductions in the construction; these coordinates can then be written:

\[\left(\frac{a^2}{c} \cdot \frac{a}{c} - \frac{bc}{a}\right), \qquad \left(\frac{b^2}{c} - \frac{a^2}{b} \cdot \frac{c}{b}\right), \qquad \left(\frac{c^2}{b} - \frac{a^2}{c} \cdot \frac{b}{c}\right).\]

We must therefore first construct the lines \(\frac{a^2}{b}\), \(\frac{c^2}{b}\), \(\frac{b^2}{c}\), \(\frac{a^2}{c}\), \(\frac{bc}{a}\) which, for brevity, I call respectively \(l_1, l_2, l_3, l_4, l_5\).

One could do construction \(\mathrm{XXXIV}^{\textit{bis}}\) five times for this, but savings are possible.

1° For all five constructions, I have to trace only three circles having centers ABC, taking them of the same radius; this will save \(\mathrm{op.} : (7C_1 + 7C_3)\).

2° To obtain \(\frac{a^2}{b}\), \(\frac{c^2}{b}\), I have to make angles A and C at B; to obtain \(\frac{b^2}{c}\), \(\frac{a^2}{c}\), angles A and B at C; to obtain \(\frac{bc}{a}\), angle C or angle B at A.

(Refer to the detail of the cited construction.)

I shall therefore take between the legs of the compass only once the chord

f. 0067 · p. 51

corresponding, in the three traced circles, to angles ABC, since these circles are traced and I shall then be able to use, for the constructions of the angles, the chord of an angle at the moment when I hold it between the legs of the compass; I shall thereby save \(\mathrm{op.} : (4C_1)\).

I have thus reduced the solution to constructing the point \(\left(l_1 \frac{a}{c} - l_5\right)\), \(\left(l_3 - l_1 \frac{c}{b}\right)\), \(\left(l_2 - l_4 \frac{b}{c}\right)\), having as yet performed only \(5[2R_1 + R_2 + 5C_1 + 3C_3] - 7C_1 - 7C_3 - 4C_1\), or \(\mathrm{op.} : [10R_1 + 5R_2 + 14C_1 + 8C_3]\).

To take the three fourth proportionals \(l_1 \cdot \frac{a}{c}\), \(l_1 \cdot \frac{c}{b}\), \(l_4 \cdot \frac{b}{c}\) which I call \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \lambda_3\), I operate thus:

Construction of \(\lambda_1\) or \(l_1 \cdot \frac{a}{c}\):

I lay off AB as CL on CB in the direction CB \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\).

I lay off \(l_1\) as CL on CA in the direction CA \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\); then I shall draw through B a parallel to LL (without tracing LL), construction XVII.

3° This parallel will cut CA at L and CL will be \(\frac{l_1 a}{c}\) or \(\lambda_1\) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)\).

Construction of \(\lambda_2\) or \(l_1 \cdot \frac{c}{b}\):

Through L, I draw a parallel to AL (without tracing AL) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)\), which cuts CB at N; CN will be \(\lambda_2\).

Construction of \(\lambda_3\) or \(l_4 \cdot \frac{b}{c}\):

I lay off \(l_4\) on CB as CP in the direction CB \(\mathrm{op.} : (3C_1 + C_3)\).

Through P, I draw a parallel to LA (without tracing LA) \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 6C_1 + 2C_3)\), which cuts CA at P; CP will be \(\lambda_3\).

I have now to construct the three lengths \(\lambda_1 - l_5\), \(l_3 - \lambda_2\), \(l_2 - \lambda_3\).

Which I do by laying off \(l_5\) on \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\) on \(l_3\), \(\lambda_3\) on \(l_2\) in the proper direction by \(\mathrm{op.} : (9C_1 + 3C_3)\), and I finally have three lengths \(\mu_1, \mu_2, \mu_3\) by the symbol \(\mathrm{op.} : (16R_1 + 8R_2 + 50C_1 + 20C_3)\).

It will only remain for me, to place W, to execute construction XLIX of the point whose proportional normal coordinates are \(\mu_1, \mu_2, \mu_3\), and I shall have obtained it by the total symbol:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(32R_1 + 16R_2 + 68C_1 + 32C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 148; \quad \text{exactitude } 100; \quad 16\text{ lines}, 32\text{ circles}.\]
f. 0068 · p. 52

b) I shall rely, to carry out the second construction, upon the means of constructing the point \(V_1 : x' x_1 (y' z_1 - z' y_1)\), etc., knowing the points \(M' : x', y', z'\) and \(M_1 : x_1, y_1, z_1\), a means which I gave at the Marseille Congress, A. F., 1891, p. 155, no. 13 [*].

One sees that if M and \(M_1\) are the Brocard points, \(V_1\) is the point \(W : \frac{a^4 - b^2 c^2}{a}\), etc., with which we are concerned.

I place the Brocard points M and \(M_1\) by construction LIV \(\mathrm{op.} : (12R_1 + 6R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3)\).

Since, in this construction, I traced only two of the straight lines AM, BM, CM and two of the straight lines \(AM_1, BM_1, CM_1\), I trace the other two, which are necessary for me here \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_1)\).

I place the point called \(\alpha\) (loco citato) \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\), and I trace the straight line \(A\alpha\), which contains W, and the straight line \(B\beta\), which also contains W \(\mathrm{op.} : (8R_1 + 4R_2)\).

W is then placed by means of the symbol:

\[\mathrm{Op.} : \left(28R_1 + 14R_2 + 12C_1 + 8C_3\right); \quad \text{simplicity } 62; \quad \text{exactitude } 40; \quad 14\text{ lines}, 8\text{ circles}.\]

And nothing says, naturally, that by relying on other properties of point W, one might not find something better.

I treated this question especially to give an example of the way to discuss problems of construction; I will add that constructions drawn from the theorems of the geometry of the triangle (such as construction b of this point W) are, so to speak, always much simpler than those deduced simply from the value of the coordinates of the point to be constructed, however much care one takes, moreover, as I have done here, to economize constructions by taking advantage of all simplifications that the nature of the data suggests.

All these very simple remarks, which are made quickly and easily as soon as one has a little habit of constructing according to our principles, are, as one sees, very long and rather tedious to detail, precisely because of their degree of obviousness; in following this memoir, pencil in hand, one will see that it reads without any effort and that almost everywhere the reader’s thought will immediately follow or even anticipate our exposition, for the knowledge required by the theory properly so called of the art of constructions is confined to the first three books of the Geometry of Legendre.

[*]

At the cited place, a few words were skipped in printing: page 155, line 4 from the bottom, one must, after Brocard points, add: \(V_1\) is the point \(\frac{a^4 - b^2 c^2}{a}\), etc.
