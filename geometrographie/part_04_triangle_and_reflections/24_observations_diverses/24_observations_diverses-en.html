Various Observations: Idealizations, Oran Notation & Replies to Mannheim and Brocard¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 52–60, fols. 0068–0076)

In Observations diverses (pp. 52–60), Lemoine defends the conceptual foundations of Géométrographie, revisits his 1888 Oran notation, and answers pointed critiques from two celebrated French geometers—Amédée Mannheim and Henri Brocard.

f. 0069 · p. 53

1. Ideal Plane & Practical Drafting Bounds (pp. 52–54). Lemoine reiterates why Géométrographie assumes an unlimited sheet and ideal instruments: just as arithmetic defines canonical algorithms independent of the width of one’s notepad, geometric simplicity must measure the intrinsic structure of a construction rather than accidental margin collisions.

f. 0070 · p. 54

At the same time, Lemoine clarifies that draftsmen must inspect acute intersections and very close pairs of points when choosing between two constructions of comparable \(S\) and \(E\)—a practical nuance expanded further in the Note complémentaire with Maurice d'Ocagne.

f. 0071 · p. 55

2. Why the 1888 Oran Eight-Symbol System Was Retired (pp. 55–56). At the 1888 Oran Congress (lemoine-1888), Lemoine had originally proposed eight fine-grained symbols (\(\rho_1, \rho_2, \rho_3, \gamma_1, \gamma'_1, \gamma''_1, \gamma_2, \gamma'_2\)) separating ruler translation, ruler rotation around a single pin, opening the compass, placing a fixed pivot, and tracing arcs.

f. 0072 · p. 56

Experience proved that splitting \(R_1\) and \(C_1\) into sub-species generated endless debates over trivial finger motions without altering the ranking of real geometric methods—whereas the five canonical operations \((R_1, R_2, C_1, C_2, C_3)\) yield an unambiguous algebraic invariant. Lemoine also surveys the extension to the draftsman’s set-square (équerre) (fully systematized in lemoine-1902).

f. 0073 · p. 57

3. Reply to :person:`Amédée Mannheim <mannheim>` — Radical Axis :math:`S = 58` vs. :math:`S = 17` (pp. 57–58). Colonel Mannheim, professor of descriptive geometry at the École Polytechnique, had objected that intuitive textbook constructions—such as finding the radical axis of two disjoint circles by joining the midpoints of their common tangents—already seem completely transparent to any geometer.

f. 0074 · p. 58

Lemoine puts Mannheim’s seemingly simple phrase to the exact Géométrographie ledger: constructing the common tangents, bisecting them, and joining the midpoints requires 9 straight lines and 12 circles (\(18R_1 + 9R_2 + 19C_1 + 12C_3\), \(S = 58, E = 37\))—more than triple the cost of Lemoine’s Problem XLI (\(10R_1 + 5R_2 + 2C_3\), \(S = 17, E = 10\), 5 lines, 2 arbitrary circles)!

4. Reply to :person:`Henri Brocard <brocard>` — Brocard Points :math:`S = 74` vs. :math:`S = 38` (pp. 58–60).

f. 0075 · p. 59

In a letter to Lemoine, Brocard proposed constructing \(\omega\) and \(\omega'\) via his own classical three-adjoint-circle method tangent to the sides of \(\triangle ABC\). Counting every perpendicular, circumcircle, and intersection line by line, Lemoine obtains \(22R_1 + 11R_2 + 24C_1 + 17C_3\) (\(S = 74, E = 46\), 11 lines, 17 circles)—nearly double Problem LV (\(S = 38, E = 24\), 6 lines, 8 circles, LIV–LV — First and Second Brocard Points omega and omega’ of a Triangle).

f. 0076 · p. 60

Concluding Observations diverses on page 60, Lemoine stresses that neither Mannheim’s nor Brocard’s objection reveals a flaw in Géométrographie; on the contrary, both prove why verbal elegance deceives even master geometers until every instrument act is counted.
