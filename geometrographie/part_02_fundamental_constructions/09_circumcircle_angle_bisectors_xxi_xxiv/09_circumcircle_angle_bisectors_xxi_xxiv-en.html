Problems XXI–XXIV: Circumcircle & Angle Bisectors¶

Émile Lemoine — La Géométrographie (English Translation, pp. 12–14, fols. 0028–0030)

XXI. — To describe a circumference passing through three given points.

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 5C_1 + 4C_3)\); simplicity 15; exactitude 9; 2 straight lines, 4 circles.

XXII. — To divide a given angle into two equal parts.

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 3C_1 + 3C_3)\); simplicity 9; exactitude 5; 1 straight line, 3 circles.

If the given angle BAC is determined by its vertex A and by two points B and C each belonging to one of the sides of the angle to be divided, the symbol of the construction would be increased by the drawing of the two straight lines AB, AC;

but one may save something and trace only one of them by operating as follows:

f. 0029 · p. 13

I trace AB \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2)\).

I describe AAC which cuts AB at C in the direction AB \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + C_3)\).

Then I describe CAC, CAC which intersect at D, and I trace AD \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 2C_1 + 2C_3)\).

AD is the bisector of the angle BAC.

In all: \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 4C_1 + 3C_3)\); simplicity 13; exactitude 8; 2 straight lines, 3 circles.

XXIII. — To divide a given arc into two equal parts.

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 2C_1 + 2C_3)\); simplicity 7; exactitude 4; 1 straight line, 2 circles.

When we give a circle or an arc of a circle, we always suppose, as in this construction, that its center is placed; if it were not, one would place it by the construction whose symbol is \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2 + 3C_2 + 3C_3)\), upon the execution of which there is no need to insist.

XXIV. — To trace the bisector of the angle formed by two straight lines AB, CD, which cannot be produced as far as their point of intersection X [*] .

From an arbitrary point A of AB, I trace (R being arbitrary) AR, which cuts CD at C and AB at B; I trace CR which cuts CD at D \(\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_2 + 2C_3)\). B and D both being on the same side of AC.

I trace BR which cuts CR at J, DR which cuts AR at I \(\mathrm{op.} : (2C_1 + 2C_3)\).

I trace AJ, CI \(\mathrm{op.} : (4R_1 + 2R_2)\). These two straight lines intersect at M, a point on the required bisector.

I trace an arbitrary circle MR which cuts AB at H, CD at G \(\mathrm{op.} : (C_1 + C_3)\), G and H being the points of intersection such that \(GX = HX\).

I take an arbitrary point M equidistant from G and H; I trace MM \(\mathrm{op.} : (2R_1 + R_2 + 2C_1 + 2C_3)\).

\(\mathrm{Op.} : (6R_1 + 3R_2 + 6C_1 + C_2 + 7C_3)\); simplicity 23; exactitude 13; 3 straight lines, 7 circles.

There is a great number of simple solutions of the same problem which may be useful; but I give only this one, which is the simplest I have found, so as not to expand our memoir unduly. This observation applies to many other problems treated here.

f. 0030 · p. 14

M is either the center of the circle inscribed in the triangle ACX, or that of the escribed circle to the same triangle tangent to the side AC, according as D and B are on one side or the other of AC (which must not be drawn). I could have continued the construction by seeking

the center \(\mu\) of that one of the two tangent circles which is not M; but the construction would have been a little more complicated, as is easy to see.

[*]

I gave in the Oran memoir already cited a solution of this problem much more complicated graphically; I had not yet trained my mind to seek graphical simplifications for their own sake, as I have already said.
